PARTICLE PHYSICS • QUARK–GLUON PLASMA • EARLY UNIVERSE

Early-Universe Matter in Smaller Nuclear Collisions

Light nuclei help physicists test how small and how cool a collision system can become while still showing plasma behavior.

ADDED NOVEMBER 3, 2026 • CERN AND BROOKHAVEN RESULTS FROM 2026

What “Big Bang matter” means

Quark–gluon plasma, or QGP, is an extreme state in which quarks and gluons are no longer confined within individual protons and neutrons. It resembles matter present during the universe’s first microseconds. A laboratory collision briefly reproduces aspects of that state; it does not reproduce the Big Bang itself.

Smaller nuclei, new tests

CERN’s LHC recorded oxygen–oxygen and neon–neon collisions in 2025. Its 2026 results show multiple QGP signatures in these smaller systems, extending studies previously centered on heavier nuclei such as lead.

The colliding beams contain ionized nuclei. Oxygen-16 has eight protons and eight neutrons; neon-20 has ten of each. Collision energy and the resulting particle patterns are more informative than a single near-light-speed percentage applied to different accelerators.

The clue: jets losing energy

High-energy quarks and gluons can produce narrow sprays of particles called jets. Passing through a hot, dense medium can reduce their energy. Researchers call this jet quenching.

ATLAS reported an imbalance between jet pairs in oxygen and neon collisions that grows in more head-on collisions. Other LHC experiments examined particle suppression and collective motion. Comparing several signatures helps test the QGP explanation against other nuclear effects.

Brookhaven tests a lower energy

In October 2026, Brookhaven reported STAR results from oxygen–oxygen collisions recorded at RHIC in 2021, at 200 GeV per nucleon pair. The analysis found about 20% suppression of high-energy particles associated with jet quenching. LHC oxygen collisions use a much higher energy, about 5.36 TeV per nucleon pair.

The two facilities therefore probe complementary conditions. RHIC’s result supports QGP formation in a small nuclear system at lower collision energy, helping constrain where the signatures may cease to appear.

What remains to learn

“Smallest” depends on the particular signature and measurements being compared. These results do not establish that every small collision forms plasma or settle the minimum possible droplet size. Earlier observations of collective flow in still smaller systems already motivated this research.

Why it belongs in MKP

MKP interpretation: The scientific advance is a controlled comparison. Changing nuclear size and collision energy tests the conditions needed for collective behavior, while different detectors provide independent ways to examine the aftermath.

Discussion questions
