2026 QQ8: Earth’s Tiny Temporary Mini-Moon

A metre-scale visitor reveals how Earth briefly captures asteroids—and how astronomers distinguish a natural rock from space junk.

ADDED NOVEMBER 21, 2026

Evidence snapshot: The Minor Planet Center’s October 29 circular reports a temporarily Earth-bound object with no identified connection to known artificial hardware. Current reporting identifies it as a natural asteroid. Its size and departure timing remain estimates.

A small companion, briefly

Earth has temporarily captured the near-Earth asteroid 2026 QQ8. Reporting places the capture in April 2026 and its size at roughly 1–2 metres across. “Mini-moon” is an informal description: this faint visitor is nothing like a second bright Moon in the night sky.

Krisztián Sárneczky discovered the object at Hungary’s Piszkéstető observatory in August. The circular’s earliest listed observation is August 19 in Universal Time; some popular accounts give August 20. Discovery and recognition of temporary capture were separate steps.

How do we know it is not space junk?

A faint moving point does not reveal its origin by appearance alone. Follow-up measurements from the Lowell Discovery Telescope, the University of Hawaii telescope and other observatories allowed astronomers to refine its trajectory.

The MPC orbital solution includes solar radiation pressure and an estimated effective area-to-mass ratio. Sunlight pushes objects slightly: an object with a large area relative to its mass responds more strongly. That provides a useful clue when comparing a compact rock with lightweight artificial debris. It is not a direct measurement of the asteroid’s interior or density.

Keep the conclusion precise: The circular reported no match to a known artificial object and encouraged further observations and dynamical work. CNN’s November 21 report describes the subsequent interpretation as confirmation of a natural origin. Neither establishes this particular rock’s original birthplace.

When will it leave?

CNN’s November 21 report gives early December 2026. Earlier reports describe a departure around the end of 2026 or early 2027. These accounts do not provide a single consistent date; MKP therefore treats the precise departure timing as unresolved here. Predictions also depend on which dynamical boundary or capture criterion is being discussed.

Visibility and safety

It is far too faint for naked-eye observing. Current reporting identifies no impact concern. A rock this small would generally be expected to break up in the atmosphere if it entered it; that hypothetical outcome is separate from an actual predicted impact.

MKP reflection: classification follows evidence

The interesting discovery is more than an extra object near Earth. Repeated observations turned an ambiguous moving point into a testable orbital interpretation. Its temporary companionship illustrates a Solar System shaped by changing gravitational encounters rather than fixed, isolated paths.

Discussion: Which observation would best distinguish a rock from a hollow rocket fragment? What would count as convincing evidence that a companion is truly bound to Earth rather than merely sharing a similar orbit around the Sun?
