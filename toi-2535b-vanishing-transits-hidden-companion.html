TOI-2535 b: The Giant Planet Whose Transits Disappeared

A changing orbital alignment explains a hot Jupiter’s vanishing transit signal, with an unseen neighboring planet implicated by its motion.

ADDED NOVEMBER 10, 2026 • UCLA ANNOUNCEMENT NOVEMBER 9

A disappearing signal

TOI-2535 b is a Jupiter-sized planet about 1,000 light-years away, orbiting its star roughly every 8.25 days. UCLA researchers Samuel Yee, Erik Petigura and colleagues studied TESS observations spanning April 2019 to March 2026. Its transits became shorter and shallower, then were no longer detected in 2026. Their timing also shifted by up to six hours.

How a planet stops transiting

A transit is the dip in starlight caused when a planet crosses its star along our line of sight. The team interprets the changing signal as orbital precession driven by an unseen neighboring planet on a tilted orbit. As the orbital orientation changes, TOI-2535 b stops crossing the star’s visible disk from our perspective.

The planet has not vanished. What disappeared was the recurring transit signal. The companion is inferred through the system’s dynamics, rather than pictured as a directly observed neighboring world.

What comes next?

UCLA reports an estimated transit return in 30–50 years, with the companion potentially transiting sooner. These are predictions, not a guaranteed observing schedule. The system may help test how hot Jupiters acquired close-in orbits while retaining nearby companions.

MKP reflection: visibility depends on geometry

This is a useful example of the difference between an object and the signal used to detect it. Losing one signal does not automatically mean losing the object. What independent evidence could distinguish a changing viewing angle from a destroyed planet or a measurement problem?

It also shows why repeated observations matter. A single transit offers a snapshot; changes in duration, depth and timing can reveal interactions that the snapshot alone would miss.
