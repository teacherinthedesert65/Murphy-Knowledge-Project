Longshot's Gas Gun: Could a Six-Mile Cannon Launch Payloads Toward Orbit?

A California startup, Longshot, is developing a gas-driven launch concept that would accelerate payloads inside a long tube rather than relying solely on conventional rocket propulsion. A Futura Sciences article shared with MKP reports roughly 100 test shots from a 59-foot apparatus and describes a proposed tube more than six miles long with a target launch speed of Mach 23.

What is being proposed?

The concept is to use a long acceleration path to impart very high velocity to a compact projectile. The shorter test apparatus is an engineering-development step, not proof that the full-scale launcher or an orbital delivery system already works. The reported number of tests, dimensions, and Mach target are claims from the cited media report; MKP has not independently verified test logs or flight data.

Why a long tube matters

For a constant-acceleration approximation, speed squared is proportional to acceleration multiplied by distance: v² = 2as. Extending the launch tube can reduce the average acceleration needed to reach a given muzzle speed. But even a tube roughly 10 kilometers long accelerating a payload to around 7.8 kilometers per second would imply an average acceleration near 3,000 m/s²—about 300 g—before accounting for losses and the actual speed of sound used in the Mach figure. That would be unsuitable for human passengers and many delicate spacecraft without extensive hardening.

Reaching speed is not the same as reaching orbit

A projectile leaving a ground-based cannon must still travel through the atmosphere. At hypersonic speeds, aerodynamic heating and drag can be severe, especially near sea level. An object also needs the right altitude, trajectory, and sustained horizontal velocity to remain in orbit. A supplementary propulsion or orbital-insertion stage may therefore be needed. A quoted Mach number by itself does not establish orbital capability.

Potential advantages—and limits

If developed successfully, ground-based acceleration might reduce the onboard propellant required for certain rugged payloads, potentially enabling different launch economics. However, structural loads, tube length and alignment, launch-site safety, atmospheric losses, projectile guidance, thermal protection, and reliable orbital insertion remain major challenges. Claims about launch cost or orbital payload capacity require demonstrated full-system results.

MKP assessment

Development status: Experimental launch technology with a proposed large-scale architecture. The reported test program is not equivalent to a demonstrated orbital launch. The idea deserves attention because it explores a genuinely different launch-energy pathway, but its practicality depends on engineering and economic evidence that has yet to be established in the source provided.
