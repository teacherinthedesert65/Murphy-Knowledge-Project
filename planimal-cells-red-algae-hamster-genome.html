“Planimal” Cells: Red-Algal DNA Persists in Cultured Mammalian Cells

Japanese researchers demonstrate long-term inheritance of algal DNA in laboratory-grown cells, a step toward exploring new cellular capabilities.

ADDED NOVEMBER 13, 2026

The new result

A Japanese-led team reported a genome-transfer experiment in Nature Communications on November 12, 2026. By fusing the red alga Cyanidioschyzon merolae with cultured mammalian cells, they produced experimental “planimal” cells. One hamster-derived line retained approximately 16.3 million base pairs of algal nuclear DNA—nearly the alga’s entire nuclear genome.

DNA inherited through cell division

The researchers found algal DNA physically linked to mammalian chromosomes. Hybrid cells continued dividing for more than 150 generations, while substantial portions of the transferred DNA persisted for 95 generations without antibiotic selection.

Approximately 96% of transferred algal protein-coding genes were not detectably expressed. The foreign DNA acquired gene-silencing marks; disrupting the methylation-related enzyme DNMT3A impaired its stable maintenance. The team describes this accommodation-and-silencing mechanism as “epigenetic gatekeeping.”

What this does—and does not—mean

These are laboratory-grown cell lines, not hybrid animals. Stable DNA inheritance does not establish that the cells gained photosynthesis or another new metabolic capability. Activating useful algal pathways remains a future goal.

How this differs from the 2024 chloroplast experiment

In an earlier study, the team introduced isolated algal chloroplasts into cultured Chinese hamster ovary cells. They detected photosynthetic electron transport for at least two days. That experiment tested functioning cellular machinery; the new study tests the long-term accommodation of foreign genetic material. Neither demonstrates an animal living on sunlight.

MKP reflection: Keeping instructions versus using them

A cell can retain genetic instructions without executing them. This distinction is central to evaluating the result: a large transferred genome is a resource for future experiments, but useful function requires appropriate regulation and compatible cellular machinery.

For a classroom discussion, this makes a concrete example of the difference between an observation and a possible application. The measured outcome is DNA retention; photosynthetic biotechnology is an ambition still requiring further evidence.

Discussion question: What would researchers need to measure to show that a transferred algal pathway supplies a useful function, rather than merely remaining present in the cell?
