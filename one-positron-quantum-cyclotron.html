ANTIMATTER • QUANTUM PHYSICS • PRECISION MEASUREMENT

One Positron, Months of Control

A tiny antimatter laboratory could help test whether nature treats matter and antimatter exactly as our best theories predict.

Published October 21, 2026 • MKP research explainer

What actually happened?

Northwestern University researchers led by Gerald Gabrielse, with Thomas G. Myers as first author, have demonstrated a one-positron quantum cyclotron. Their paper appeared in Physical Review Letters on October 21, 2026. A positron is the electron’s antimatter counterpart. The team isolated one in a Penning trap and resolved its lowest cyclotron and spin energy states. [1]

The important advance is the degree of quantum control, beyond simply keeping antimatter confined. The paper reports that a single positron has been stored for months. This system creates the conditions for a much more precise measurement of its magnetic moment. [1]

How do you hold antimatter?

Contact with an electron can annihilate a positron, so an ordinary container will not do. Electric and magnetic fields hold the particle away from material surfaces, while an exceptionally good vacuum reduces collisions. Northwestern describes a trap cooled to about 0.3 kelvin above absolute zero and positrons obtained from radioactive sodium-22. Carefully tuned microwaves allow researchers to change and detect quantum energy states. [2]

Think of the allowed energies as steps on a staircase: the experiment distinguishes individual steps instead of averaging over a broad range of motion. That analogy describes discrete energies; it does not mean the particle is a miniature ball sitting still.

Why does one particle matter?

A magnetic moment describes how a particle responds magnetically. Comparing the positron’s magnetic properties with the electron’s offers a test of CPT symmetry—a foundational principle combining charge conjugation, spatial inversion and time reversal. The prediction is that their magnetic moments have equal magnitudes and opposite signs. [1]

If a future comparison reveals a reproducible disagreement after experimental effects are ruled out, it could challenge a basic assumption of established physics. If the measurements agree, they will place tighter limits on possible departures. Either outcome improves what we know.

What this result does—and does not—show

Demonstrated: a trapped positron with resolved quantum states. Next goal: use the apparatus for a sharper magnetic-moment comparison. The current paper does not establish CPT violation, explain why the universe contains more matter than antimatter, or demonstrate an antimatter power source. It is a precision-measurement advance. [1]

Why MKP is following it

This is a useful example of how engineering makes fundamental questions experimentally accessible. An isolated particle, a cold environment and sensitive detection can become a laboratory for testing laws that apply across the universe.
