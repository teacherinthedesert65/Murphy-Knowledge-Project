Thailand’s Giant Fossil Rodent: Yakamys sirindhornae

Two fossil teeth reveal an unusually large early rodent. Its estimated size invites a capybara comparison, but its family and appearance need careful distinction.

ADDED NOVEMBER 6, 2026 • STUDY PUBLISHED NOVEMBER 4, 2026

A giant among early rodents

A November 4, 2026 Scientific Reports study describes Yakamys sirindhornae, a new genus and species from the Krabi Basin in southern Thailand. Its late Eocene deposits date to approximately 35.1–33.7 million years ago.

The evidence consists of a lower second molar and an associated fragmentary lower incisor. Their large dimensions, tooth anatomy and enamel structure support assignment to the extinct rodent family Ischyromyidae.

How big was it?

Seven tooth-based equations produce estimates from 21.4 to 47.3 kilograms. The researchers use roughly 30 kilograms—about 66 pounds—as a rounded summary, not a measured weight. The molar is about 11.6 millimetres long.

The capybara comparison

Yes, capybaras are rodents too. “Rodent” is a broad classification encompassing very different animals; sharing that label does not make two species the same kind of rodent.

A reconstruction is not a complete fossil. These teeth do not establish the animal’s fur, face, body outline or swimming behavior. A capybara-like illustration can help imagine a large rodent, but resemblance in artwork is not evidence of close relationship.

Why the discovery matters

The study documents substantial body-size increase in an early Southeast Asian rodent lineage, long before the better-known giant rodents of South America. The species name honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn’s support of science and paleontology.

Reading fragmentary fossils

The useful distinction is between a specimen, an estimate and an illustration. The teeth are specimens that researchers can compare directly. Body mass is an inference from a model. A full-body image adds visual choices that the preserved material cannot independently confirm.

That does not make a reconstruction useless. It makes the label important. A good question for any fossil portrait is: which features come from the fossil, which come from comparisons, and which remain artistic interpretation?
