Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua: A Remarkably Close Quasar Pair at Cosmic Dawn

JWST and Gemini observations reveal a compelling early quasar-pair candidate, testing ideas about rapid black-hole growth and dense young galaxies.

ADDED NOVEMBER 12, 2026

Two bright engines in the young Universe

J1602+4228AB, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, is observed at redshift about 6.08, when the Universe was roughly 900 million years old. Silvia Onorato and colleagues studied the system with JWST’s Aether survey and Gemini North spectroscopy.

The inferred black-hole masses are approximately 1.5 billion and 400 million solar masses. Their projected separation is about 5.56 kiloparsecs—18,100 light-years—making this an exceptionally compact quasar-pair candidate from the Epoch of Reionization.

Why the distinction between pair and candidate matters

Spectral differences favor two physically distinct quasars. However, the institutional announcement notes that a lens model is still needed to definitively exclude gravitational lensing: multiple apparent images of one background source.

Keep “closest” in context: The compactness claim concerns quasars at cosmic dawn, not every black-hole pair across all cosmic history. The quoted separation is a projection on the sky, not a complete three-dimensional distance.

A test of early growth

The authors suggest that such a close pair could trace an unusually dense environment or merger-associated activity. This offers evidence for investigating how massive galaxies and their central black holes assembled so early.

MKP reflection

A striking discovery is most useful when it sharpens a test. Here, two questions should remain separate: are there definitely two distinct active black holes, and what growth history could account for their inferred masses? Stronger evidence for the first would constrain—but would not automatically settle—the second.

What observation would be most decisive next: resolving the host galaxies, mapping surrounding gas, searching for a foreground lens, or measuring more close pairs? Each would address a different uncertainty.
