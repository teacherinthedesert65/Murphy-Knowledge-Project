A Feathered Dinosaur and the Branching Origins of Flight

What can preserved feathers tell us about the paths evolution took into the air?

ADDED OCTOBER 6, 2026 • STUDY PUBLISHED SEPTEMBER 29, 2026

The discovery

Researchers described Norellraptor barsboldi, a small predatory dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous Jiufotang Formation in Liaoning, northeastern China. Its complete, 57-centimetre skeleton preserves portions of its feathers. Bone analysis indicates that it was at least three years old when it died.

Background: comparing evolutionary branches

It belongs to the microraptorines, feathered relatives of birds. Some members carried long feathers on both front and hind limbs. Researchers compared anatomical changes across an evolutionary tree to investigate how flight-related structures developed.

Why it matters

About 30 percent of the changes identified in microraptorine evolution also appeared in the bird lineage, but in a different sequence. This supports the interpretation that their flight apparatus developed independently under different selective pressures.

Evidence, not final proof

The fossil strengthens a hypothesis; it does not settle every question about the origin of flight or establish exactly how this animal flew. Additional fossils and bone-growth studies are needed to test the broader pattern.

Discussion

MKP invites readers to consider how scientists distinguish a fossil's observable features from explanations of its behavior and evolutionary history.
