Ryugu’s Rocks Record a Surprisingly Late Episode of Water

Returned samples reveal ancient fluid movement more than a billion years after Ryugu’s parent asteroid formed.

ADDED NOVEMBER 4, 2026 • FEATURED STUDY PUBLISHED SEPTEMBER 10, 2025

An asteroid’s chemical memory

Samples returned by Japan’s Hayabusa2 mission preserve evidence of water reacting with rock in Ryugu’s ancient parent body. A 2025 Nature study extends that history: fluid moved through this material more than one billion years after the parent asteroid formed.

This is evidence of a late episode, rather than proof that liquid water flowed continuously for a billion years.

Reading the lutetium–hafnium record

The researchers measured the radioactive lutetium-176 to hafnium-176 system in the samples. Their results indicate late movement of lutetium by fluid, preserving a chemical signature of water activity well beyond the earliest phase of asteroid evolution.

The team proposes that an impact melted ice retained inside the parent body and allowed water to escape. That explanation connects the isotope evidence with a possible heating event; the collision itself was not directly observed.

How water changes asteroid rock

Earlier sample research, published in Science in 2022, identified abundant phyllosilicates and carbonates formed through aqueous alteration: chemical reactions between liquid water and the original minerals. It also found carbon-dioxide-bearing water inclusions within a pyrrhotite crystal.

These mineral clues support formation of the parent asteroid in the outer solar system. The 2022 analysis modeled formation around two million years after the solar system began, followed by low-temperature, alkaline water–rock reactions.

“Dry” needs qualification. Ancient fluid flow, water trapped in microscopic inclusions, and water chemically incorporated into minerals describe different things. None of these findings demonstrates rivers or accessible liquid reservoirs on present-day Ryugu.

Why it matters for Earth

The late-flow result suggests that primitive bodies could preserve ice longer than a simple early-heating model might imply. The research team argues that ice, alongside hydrated minerals, could increase estimates of the water carried by carbonaceous material that helped build Earth.

It does not establish that Ryugu supplied Earth’s oceans, or settle how much water came from each source. Its value is a better record of how potential water-delivery material evolved.

Questions to explore

How can an isotope signature distinguish an early reaction from later fluid movement? What other measurements could test the impact-melting explanation? Comparing the chemical clock with mineral textures is a useful way to separate measurements from a reconstructed history.
