AI in Drug Discovery & Delivery: From Algorithms to Patients

Can AI help scientists find safer medicines, design new antibiotics, deliver treatments precisely, and improve access to healthcare? Here is what has actually been demonstrated—and what remains to be proved.

The Murphy Knowledge Project • Reviewed October 13, 2026 • Research explainer

A promising medicine must do far more than look convincing in a computer simulation. It must be made, tested in biological systems, evaluated for toxicity, compared rigorously in people, manufactured reliably, and delivered to patients who need it. Artificial intelligence can help at several stages—but it is not a substitute for evidence or human medical judgment.

DemonstratedAI can guide candidate molecule design, identify biological patterns, and help optimize experimental formulations.
In clinical testingSome AI-assisted drug candidates have reached human trials, including a Phase 3 study initiated in 2026.
Not establishedThat AI can bypass clinical trials, guarantee safer drugs, or automatically make new treatments affordable.

1. What does AI actually contribute to drug development?

Researchers use different forms of AI for different questions. Machine-learning systems may predict protein structures and molecular interactions, search for patterns in disease data, rank chemical candidates, propose entirely new molecules, or estimate promising conditions for manufacturing and drug delivery.

The process remains iterative: researchers choose a disease question, collect and assess data, use models to propose experiments, perform laboratory studies, inspect failures, and refine the models. A model's confidence score is not clinical evidence. Predictions can fail when biological complexity, biased data, toxicity, metabolism, or real patient differences enter the picture.

2. Case study: an AI-assisted drug reaches Phase 3

Rentosertib (formerly ISM001-055) is an experimental inhibitor of TNIK developed for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, a progressive disease that scars lung tissue. Generative AI helped identify the drug target and candidate molecule.

A peer-reviewed, randomized Phase 2a study published on June 3, 2025 reported results for 71 participants across treatment and placebo groups during 12 weeks. The highest-dose group showed a mean change in forced vital capacity of +98.4 mL, versus −20.3 mL in the placebo group. Those findings were encouraging but based on small groups and short follow-up. Treatment-emergent adverse events were common, and some discontinuations were associated with liver-related effects or diarrhea. Read the Phase 2a study in Nature Medicine →

On September 10, 2026, the developer announced that the first participant had been dosed in a Phase 3 trial, GENESIS-IPF-3, intended to study efficacy and safety over 52 weeks. This milestone is a company announcement about trial progress, not proof of efficacy or an approval to prescribe the drug. Insilico Medicine's dated Phase 3 announcement →

Why the distinction matters: An AI-discovered candidate reaching Phase 3 is a notable research milestone, but successful approval and long-term benefit remain unproven. Patients should not treat rentosertib as an available, established therapy.

3. Case study: designing antibiotics against resistant bacteria

Researchers at MIT reported in August 2025 that generative AI systems created and screened more than 36 million proposed compounds for activity against drug-resistant bacteria. From a narrowed set targeting Staphylococcus aureus, researchers synthesized and tested 22 candidates; six showed strong activity in laboratory cultures. A leading candidate, DN1, also cleared a resistant-bacterial skin infection in a mouse model.

That is a meaningful result in early-stage discovery, especially because the designs explore chemical structures outside conventional libraries. It is not evidence of a safe or effective new antibiotic for people; human studies, manufacturing and regulatory review would still be necessary. MIT's original August 14, 2025 report →

The applied goal is important: drug-resistant infections threaten patients worldwide, and new antibiotic classes are difficult to develop. AI may help enlarge the pool of scientifically testable possibilities, but stewardship and resistance monitoring would remain essential even for a successful medicine.

4. The next challenge: getting the right medicine to the right place

Discovering a promising molecule is only one part of the problem. A therapy may fail because it breaks down too soon, cannot reach its intended tissue, causes harmful effects elsewhere, or requires an impractical dose. Drug-delivery engineering tries to solve these problems.

One active field is lipid nanoparticles—tiny lipid-based carriers that can deliver nucleic acids and other payloads. Their behavior depends on the composition, structure and relative amounts of ingredients. Machine learning can help researchers screen combinations and predict characteristics such as cellular uptake, toxicity and tissue distribution.

A peer-reviewed Nature Reviews Bioengineering article published September 17, 2026 reviews how AI and machine learning are being used for nanoparticle formulation and preclinical predictions. Its emphasis is on development methods and opportunities, not proof that AI-designed particles reliably target any organ in human patients. 2026 review: AI and nanoparticle drug-delivery systems →

A related September 29, 2026 commentary warns that AI-guided lipid nanoparticle development still needs to incorporate immunological effects more fully. A carrier that reaches a target may nevertheless trigger unwanted immune reactions. 2026 commentary: the immunology challenge →

5. Medicines and intelligent delivery devices are different categories

An AI-assisted drug is a medicine whose discovery or development used AI. An algorithm-controlled delivery device is a system that uses sensor readings and software to adjust an administered treatment. They may eventually intersect, but they should not be conflated.

For example, automated insulin-delivery devices already use computer-controlled algorithms, continuous glucose monitoring, and pumps to adjust insulin delivery for some people with diabetes. These are genuine examples of clinically used adaptive treatment systems; they do not mean that the insulin itself was designed by generative AI or that every insulin pump employs modern machine-learning methods. FDA: How artificial pancreas systems work →

6. Could AI change clinical trials?

AI models may assist with identifying study populations, detecting patterns in safety data, selecting candidate doses or evaluating possible trial designs. Some developers are studying simulated or 'virtual' patients, but simulations are only as sound as their assumptions and training data. A model that predicts a trial result does not, by itself, replace the obligation to demonstrate benefit and risk in appropriately designed human studies.

In January 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency jointly published 10 guiding principles for responsible use of AI across the medicine lifecycle. Their shared emphasis includes transparency, appropriate data, risk management, model assessment and oversight. A separate FDA document issued in January 2025 on AI in regulatory submissions is explicitly a draft guidance, not a finalized binding rule. FDA: AI in drug development and guidance →

7. Real-world implications for patients and society

Rare diseases. Faster target discovery could expand investigations into diseases that historically attracted limited investment. This is an opportunity, not a guarantee: specialized clinical trials, small patient populations and commercial constraints still affect whether a medicine reaches patients.

Personalized treatment. AI might eventually make it easier to match therapies and delivery systems to a person's biomarkers or disease subtype. Researchers must verify that performance holds across age groups, sexes, ancestry groups, coexisting conditions and settings underrepresented in training data.

Accessibility and price. Lower research costs do not automatically translate to lower medication prices. Patents, manufacturing, clinical testing, distribution, reimbursement and market incentives all matter. MKP should ask who actually experiences the savings.

Patient safety. An apparently precise algorithm may still fail because of incomplete data, hidden biases, novel adverse effects or distribution shifts. Researchers should report failed predictions and negative results—not just successful AI demonstrations.

Human expertise. Chemists, pharmacologists, physicians, statisticians, clinical trial volunteers, regulators and patient communities remain central. Responsible AI should enlarge their ability to discover and assess therapies, not diminish the need for independent review.

8. A practical checklist for evaluating AI-medicine headlines

  • What actually happened? A computer prediction, a synthesized compound, animal testing, a small human trial or an approved treatment?
  • Who reported it? An independent peer-reviewed study, regulator, trial registry or developer's press release?
  • What was measured? Cell activity, animal response, a surrogate endpoint or a meaningful clinical outcome?
  • How large and long? What were the sample size, comparison group, follow-up and uncertainty?
  • What were the harms? What adverse events, dosing challenges, off-target effects and incomplete data were reported?
  • Who benefits? Will patients gain access, or will the discovery remain inaccessible because of price or infrastructure?

A robust MKP assessment should give readers enough information to distinguish a compelling technological demonstration from a clinically established benefit.

OPEN DISCUSSION • CHALLENGE IDEAS, NOT PEOPLE

What kind of AI-assisted healthcare future should we build?

MKP welcomes scientific, ethical, medical and community perspectives, including disagreement. Please distinguish observed results from projections, avoid sharing private medical records, and disagree respectfully without attacking people.

  1. Speed versus evidence: If AI finds new compounds rapidly, what evidence must still be required before patients can receive them?
  2. Precision versus fairness: Could AI-guided drugs and delivery systems widen health disparities if training data or access are unequal?
  3. Affordability: What would demonstrate that AI actually reduced the price patients pay, not just the cost of research?
  4. Accountability: When an AI recommendation contributes to a harmful clinical or research decision, who should be responsible?
  5. Human–AI synergy: Which decisions belong with researchers, clinicians, patients and regulators, and which could responsibly be delegated to software?
