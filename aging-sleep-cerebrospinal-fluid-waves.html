Aging and Sleep: Smaller Cerebrospinal Fluid Waves in Older Adults

MIT research links reduced sleep-related brain fluid waves to changes in slow brain activity and blood-vessel responses.

ADDED NOVEMBER 13, 2026

What the study measured

A November 13, 2026, study in Nature Aging reports reduced cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow waves during non-REM sleep in older adults. Sydney Bailes and Stephanie Williams are the lead authors, with MIT’s Laura Lewis as senior author.

The researchers combined EEG measurements of electrical brain activity with fast functional MRI measurements of hemodynamics and CSF inflow. They compared young adults aged 18–39 with older adults aged 60–84 during nighttime scanning.

Two linked pathways

Reduced CSF waves were associated with weaker EEG slow waves, smaller global hemodynamic oscillations, and gray matter atrophy. Experiments during wakefulness used visual stimulation and breath-holding to probe neural and vascular responses. The findings support contributions from both altered brain activity and altered cerebrovascular physiology.

Individuals varied

MIT reports that some older participants retained CSF waves within the younger group’s range. None had a diagnosed neurodegenerative disorder. The group difference therefore should not be read as an inevitable outcome for every older adult.

Fluid motion is not a direct waste-clearance measurement

The experiment measured CSF inflow signals and related physiology. It did not directly quantify removal of amyloid, tau, or other waste proteins, prove that reduced waves cause dementia, or establish a treatment that prevents cognitive decline.

What comes next?

The team hopes to study whether these changes predict later brain-health outcomes and whether interventions can improve CSF flow. MIT notes that a separate pink-noise experiment increased flow in younger adults; the approach had not yet been tested in older adults. That is a research lead, not an established dementia-prevention treatment.

MKP reflection

Sleep duration is only one dimension of sleep biology. This work illustrates how electrical activity, vascular responses, and fluid movement can be investigated together. It also shows why a promising physiological signal needs further testing before becoming a clinical recommendation.

Discussion question: What would a follow-up study need to measure to connect improved fluid flow with a meaningful improvement in brain health?
