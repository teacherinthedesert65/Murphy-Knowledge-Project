Hunting “Ghost Particles” Beneath Lake Superior

The END experiment tests whether underwater sensors can distinguish Fermilab beam neutrinos from background signals in natural lake water.

ADDED NOVEMBER 12, 2026

A lake becomes a particle-physics laboratory

The Experimental Neutrino Detector (END) project, led by the University of Minnesota Duluth, aims to observe neutrinos from Fermilab’s existing NuMI beam in Lake Superior near Two Harbors. A related experiment in Lake Vermilion tests a different detector design. The university’s announced testing window runs from autumn 2026 to approximately April 2027.

How can sensors see an almost invisible particle?

Neutrinos have no electric charge and very small masses. They interact with matter so rarely that enormous numbers pass through us unnoticed. They are among the universe’s most abundant particles; photons are more numerous.

The sensors do not photograph neutrinos themselves. A rare interaction can produce fast charged particles. If those particles travel faster than light travels through water, they emit Cherenkov light—a faint optical signal. This does not exceed light’s speed in a vacuum.

Why this location and design?

The NuMI beam already crosses the lakes on its route toward northern Minnesota. Lake Superior’s photosensors will look for interaction light at night, because daylight would overwhelm the faint signals. Lake Vermilion’s light-tight scintillator detectors use plastic that flashes when traversed by charged particles and can operate around the clock. Separating beam-related events from cosmic-ray and other backgrounds is a central goal.

Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution designed five large support frames and is deploying them with the research vessel Neil Armstrong. The plan places the arrays on the lakebed and connects each to shore with fiber-optic cable. END is sponsored by DARPA in cooperation with the Department of Energy and national laboratories.

A feasibility test, not a discovery announcement

Natural-water and ice neutrino observatories already exist. END’s focus is demonstrating detection of an accelerator-produced beam in natural lake water. The deployment announcements do not establish that the experiment has already detected that beam successfully.

MKP reflection: A known source helps test a new instrument

A controlled beam gives researchers something specific to look for while they evaluate an unfamiliar detector environment. The challenge is not simply seeing a flash: it is showing that the flash belongs to the intended signal.

Discussion question: What evidence would convince you that an underwater event came from the accelerator beam rather than an unrelated background particle?
