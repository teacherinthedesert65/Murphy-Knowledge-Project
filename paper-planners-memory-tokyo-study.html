Paper Planners and Memory: What the Tokyo Study Found

A 2021 experiment compared paper, tablets and phones during schedule recall—revealing brain-activity differences with important limits.

ADDED NOVEMBER 7, 2026 • STUDY PUBLISHED MARCH 19, 2021

The experiment behind the headline

University of Tokyo researchers and collaborators studied 48 adults aged 18–29. Participants recorded a fictional schedule using a paper datebook and pen, a tablet with a stylus, or a smartphone with a touchscreen keyboard. After about an hour, they answered memory questions while undergoing functional MRI.

The paper was published on March 19, 2021 in Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience. Recent coverage revisits that experiment; it is not a newly conducted 2026 trial.

What changed

The paper group entered the schedule more quickly and performed better on straightforward questions. During recall, it also showed greater activation in areas including the hippocampus, precuneus, visual cortex and language-related frontal regions. Overall task performance, however, was similar across groups.

The authors propose that a physical page provides richer spatial and contextual cues for remembering where information appeared. This is an interpretation of the findings, rather than proof of one isolated mechanism.

What the study cannot settle

More brain activation is not a general score for better thinking. The study measured a particular short-term scheduling task in a small young-adult sample. It does not establish that paper always improves memory or that phones damage it.

The tablet group also wrote by hand. Consequently, the comparison is not simply handwriting versus typing: the devices, interfaces and physical layouts differed too.

The study did not test schoolchildren, long-term classroom learning, ADHD treatment or whether reminders prevent missed real-world appointments. Those are separate questions.

A useful distinction for everyday planning

Remembering information without assistance and successfully managing appointments are different goals. An alarm can help someone arrive on time even if they cannot recall a date unaided. A paper page can provide a visible overview without providing an automatic alert.

A reasonable practical experiment is to compare methods on the outcome that matters: accurate recall, completed tasks, missed appointments or ease of reviewing the week. A combination of paper planning and digital reminders is also an option. These are practical suggestions, not outcomes demonstrated by this study.

Questions for learners

Does a stable page layout help you remember where you wrote something? Can you recall your plan before checking it? What happens when the same method is tried across several weeks? Measuring those outcomes is more useful than assuming one format must suit everyone.
