HISTORY • LITERATURE • ANCIENT MESOPOTAMIA

Enheduanna: Priestess, Poet, and the First Known Author by Name

A voice from ancient Mesopotamia opens questions about women’s history, literary authorship, and how texts survive.

ADDED NOVEMBER 16, 2026

A named voice from ancient Iraq

About 4,300 years ago, Enheduanna held the powerful office of high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur, in what is now southern Iraq. A daughter of Sargon of Akkad, she lived around the twenty-third century BCE. Her position brought religious authority and political influence.

The Morgan Library & Museum describes her as history’s first author known by name. Works associated with her include The Exaltation of Inanna and the Temple Hymns, a collection celebrating Mesopotamian sanctuaries. The collection’s ending names Enheduanna as its compiler.

What does “she signed her hymns” mean?

That headline compresses a more complicated record. A named attribution within a transmitted text is not the same thing as a surviving autograph written by its author’s own hand. Nor does “first known by name” mean she invented writing or that no one composed literature before her.

Most surviving manuscripts of the poems attributed to Enheduanna date to roughly the eighteenth century BCE, about five centuries after her lifetime. Scholars disagree over whether they preserve her compositions, later reworkings, or works written in her name. Changes in language may reflect later composition or scribes updating older material. The Temple Hymns also contain later additions.

An artifact anchors the historical person

The Disk of Enheduanna, held by the Penn Museum, preserves her name and a ritual scene identifying her elevated status. It is physical evidence for the priestess and her office, rather than an autograph manuscript of the poems. Separating those kinds of evidence helps us appreciate both the historical woman and the later literary tradition.

MKP reflection

Enheduanna expands the familiar starting point of literary history beyond Greece and Rome. Her story also invites a broader question: whose words get copied, preserved, translated, and taught? Acknowledging uncertainty in attribution does not erase her place in the history of women, religious authority, and literature.

Discussion question

If a poem survives only in copies made centuries later, what evidence would persuade you that its named author composed it—and what might later editors have contributed?
