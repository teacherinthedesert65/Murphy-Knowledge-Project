Bee Flies: Fuzzy Bee Mimics With a Surprising Life Cycle

These hovering, flower-visiting flies resemble bees, but their wings, mouthparts, and larval lives tell a different story.

ADDED NOVEMBER 13, 2026

A fly wearing a bee-like disguise

That tiny, furry insect hovering at a flower may be a bee fly. Many members of the bee-fly family, Bombyliidae, resemble bees. In the genus Bombylius, the resemblance includes a hairy body and a bee-like appearance that can help deter predators.

Bee flies have one pair of wings; bees have two pairs. Their long, projecting mouthpart is used to drink nectar. It is not a stinger, and bee flies do not bite or sting people.

What about the “Japanese bee fly”?

Bee flies do occur in Japan. TAKAO 599 MUSEUM describes a yellow-brown, velvet-haired Bombylius bee fly found there, with adults visiting flowers and hovering in spring. However, a social-media caption is not enough to identify a particular species. The exact species, location, and photographic provenance of the image that inspired this feature have not been verified here.

Flower visitors with a very different childhood

Adult Bombylius feed mainly on nectar and can pollinate flowers. Their larvae have a different ecological role: many develop as parasitoids associated with solitary bees. In Bombylius major, females place eggs near host nests; the young larvae seek a host larva to parasitize. A parasitoid ultimately kills its host during development.

Resemblance is a clue, not an identification

Looking like a bee does not make an insect a bee. Wing number, antennae, mouthparts, and other anatomical details offer better evidence than a fuzzy silhouette. Precise species identification may require views that an appealing social-media photograph does not provide.

MKP reflection

The same insect can help flowering plants as an adult while exploiting another insect during its larval stage. Ecology becomes more interesting when we follow the whole life cycle instead of assigning a creature one simple role.

Discussion question: How might a harmless insect benefit when a predator mistakes it for a stinging one?
