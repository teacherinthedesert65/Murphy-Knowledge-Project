Should AI Developers Agree to Capability Limits? Jakub Pachocki's Perplexity Proposal

On November 22, 2026, OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki published Perplexity and Intelligence, an essay advocating greater transparency about AI pretraining progress and a common approach to development limits across leading AI laboratories. He argues that the ability of a model to predict unseen text—measured through perplexity or cross-entropy loss—can help track the underlying progress of AI systems and inform shared safety standards.

What is perplexity?

Perplexity is a measure of how well a probabilistic language model predicts unseen text. Lower perplexity generally indicates more accurate predictions on a particular evaluation dataset. Researchers often use cross-entropy loss, the logarithmic counterpart of perplexity. This is not a direct measurement of consciousness, humanlike understanding, or every form of intelligence.

What does Pachocki propose?

The proposal calls for developers to disclose comparable measures of pretraining progress, agree on an initial development limit, and commit not to pass that limit until concrete safety and monitoring milestones have been achieved. A key motivation is the concern that AI capabilities could accelerate faster than the tools available to interpret, supervise, and control advanced systems. The essay is an argument for voluntary industry coordination, not a binding regulation or an agreement already adopted by competing laboratories.

Why release internal measurements?

OpenAI disclosed measurements from held-out evaluation material derived from its internal codebase, showing progress across generations of its models. Pachocki acknowledges that publishing such information may reveal competitive details but argues that establishing common safety practices is more important. The measurements are informative about OpenAI's internal trajectory; comparing them directly with results from other companies would require a consistent evaluation setup.

What are the limitations?

One metric cannot capture every AI risk or capability. Perplexity depends on the dataset and testing methodology. Systems can improve at complex tasks through post-training, reasoning procedures, tool use, and other changes that are not fully represented by base-model next-token prediction. Shared limits would also raise practical questions: who sets thresholds, verifies measurements, evaluates monitoring quality, and enforces commitments?

MKP analysis: the scientific and governance stakes

A shared, auditable measurement could make otherwise opaque progress easier to discuss and could support coordinated safety commitments. But it should be used alongside direct evaluations of dangerous capabilities, misuse potential, reliability, transparency, and real-world impacts. A voluntary cap could be meaningful if participants agree on common tests and credible verification; without those elements, it risks being more symbolic than operational.

The central question is not simply how fast models improve, but whether the ability to understand and monitor their behavior can keep pace.

What to watch next

Important developments would include other frontier developers endorsing common limits, the creation of shared evaluation datasets, independent auditing mechanisms, and explicit safety milestones that must be met before limits are raised.
