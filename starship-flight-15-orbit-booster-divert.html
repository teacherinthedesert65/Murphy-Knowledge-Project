Starship Flight 15: Orbit and Satellite Deployment, but No Booster Catch

A dated mission update separates Flight 15’s orbital achievements from its unsuccessful booster recovery and still-unconfirmed upper-stage return.

ADDED NOVEMBER 14, 2026

Two stages, different outcomes

Starship Flight 15 launched from Starbase, Texas, on November 13, 2026. The upper stage reached orbit and deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites. Super Heavy diverted from its planned tower catch and was lost in an explosive offshore return in the Gulf of Mexico. The planned recovery of both stages therefore did not occur.

What the mission was designed to test

SpaceX’s mission plan called for an approximately 275-kilometer orbit, about 14 circuits of Earth, and a flight lasting more than 22 hours before a possible return to Starbase. Catch attempts were conditional on vehicle and ground-system readiness. Planned milestones should be distinguished from completed events.

What caused the booster abort?

In a November 14 update reported from SpaceX’s webcast, spokesperson Dan Huot clarified that guidance, navigation, and control performed as expected: it detected that the booster was outside the required position for a catch and commanded an offshore diversion. The underlying reason the booster reached that position remained under investigation. An abort response and the fault that triggered it are separate questions.

Status at this article’s preparation

This November 14 update confirms the launch, payload deployment, and unsuccessful booster recovery. It does not confirm an upper-stage catch or a successful full orbital round trip. Available reports still described the ship’s return as pending. Consult the linked mission and live-report pages for later developments.

MKP reflection: Score the objectives separately

Launch systems must accomplish several tasks: reach the intended trajectory, release payloads, survive return, recover hardware, and eventually fly that hardware again. Success at one stage does not establish success at the others. Nor does one failure erase the data obtained from the rest of the flight.
