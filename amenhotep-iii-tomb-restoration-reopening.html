Amenhotep III’s Tomb: Reopening After Decades of Conservation

Egyptian and Japanese specialists restored a royal tomb in Luxor’s Western Valley. The reopening took place in October 2025.

ADDED NOVEMBER 6, 2026 • REOPENING: OCTOBER 2025

A royal tomb returns to view

The tomb of Amenhotep III, commonly designated KV22 or WV22, reopened to visitors in October 2025 after a conservation programme spanning more than two decades. UNESCO’s March 2026 account confirms the date. Recent coverage is revisiting that event.

The approximately 3,400-year-old monument lies in the Western Valley of the Valley of the Kings, within the World Heritage site Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis.

Why the work took so long

UNESCO describes structural instability, salt crystallization, microbiological risks and environmental changes threatening the tomb’s paintings and architecture. Egyptian and Japanese specialists collaborated on three conservation phases beginning in 2001.

Work included stabilizing fractured pillars, treating cracks, reattaching limestone fragments, and cleaning and securing painted surfaces. The guiding approach emphasized stabilization with minimal intervention.

Technology helps protect ancient surfaces

Laser scanning and photogrammetry created a digital model for future comparisons. Sensors monitor conditions including temperature, humidity, carbon dioxide and structural deformation. Improved lighting and visitor interpretation accompanied the reopening.

Reopening is a milestone, not the end of conservation. UNESCO calls for continued monitoring, reassessment and careful visitor management. A restored tomb still contains fragile ancient material.

What restoration lets us ask

The headline celebrates a doorway opening, but the deeper story is the work that makes access possible. Conservators must decide how to stabilize damaged material, document their interventions and distinguish surviving evidence from later repair.

Digital records are especially useful when repeated measurements can reveal change. A detailed scan provides a reference, while monitoring helps teams decide when conditions require attention. Neither replaces the original monument or the expertise needed to interpret it.

Discussion

How should a historic site balance public access with preservation? Which changes should trigger a reduction in visitor numbers? What should an interpretation panel tell visitors about restoration? These questions connect ancient history with present-day scientific judgment.
