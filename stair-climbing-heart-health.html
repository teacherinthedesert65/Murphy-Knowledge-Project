Stair Climbing and Heart Health: Five Flights, 50 Steps, and the Limits of a Headline

Could a small daily habit be associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease? A large prospective study suggests yes—but the difference between an association and proof of prevention matters.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • OCTOBER 14, 2026

What the research actually found

In an analysis of 458,860 UK Biobank adults, people reporting more than five flights of stairs per day (roughly 50 stair steps) had an approximately 20% or greater lower relative rate of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) than people who reported no daily stair climbing, after statistical adjustment for measured risk factors. The outcome included coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke—not simply heart attacks alone.

Evidence level: prospective observational cohort. The result is an association, not proof that prescribing five flights will reduce an individual's heart attack risk by 20%.

The study behind the claim

Song and colleagues published their analysis in Atherosclerosis in 2023, using UK Biobank data from 458,860 adults and a median 12.5 years of follow-up. Investigators documented 39,043 ASCVD outcomes. People reported their daily stair use, which was grouped into levels and compared with those reporting no stair use. The research team adjusted for several demographic, behavioral, and medical variables.

The adjusted hazard ratios for ASCVD compared with no stair climbing were 0.97 for 1–5 flights per day; 0.84 for 6–10; 0.78 for 11–15; 0.77 for 16–20; and 0.81 for 21 or more. A hazard ratio of 0.78 represents about a 22% lower relative hazard in that group, not a 22-percentage-point absolute reduction.

The strongest association was not at the very highest stair-use category. This does not show that more stairs are harmful; the groups may differ in ways that statistics do not fully capture.

Why stairs might matter

Walking upstairs is a weight-bearing activity that engages large muscle groups and often raises heart rate faster than level walking. It can provide a brief bout of aerobic effort during ordinary daily life, with no special equipment. Repeated physical activity supports cardiovascular fitness and is part of established heart-health guidance.

That explanation is biologically plausible, but this particular study did not randomly assign people to climb a set number of stairs or show that fitness changes caused the lower disease rates.

Does this mean “forget 10,000 steps”?

No. The headline creates a competition the research did not test. This study did not directly compare a program of stair climbing with a program of walking 10,000 daily steps. There is no credible conversion showing that 50 stair steps are medically equivalent to 10,000 level steps, and 10,000 is not a universal minimum required for good health.

A better interpretation is that short bouts of movement can complement an overall activity routine. Walking, cycling, swimming, and other accessible activities remain useful options. What matters is safe, sustainable movement suited to a person's capacity.

What are the limitations?

  • Correlation is not causation. People who already feel healthy may be more likely to use stairs, while people with pain, disability, limited mobility, or heart disease may avoid them (reverse causation).
  • Stair use was self-reported. Counts and intensity can be imprecise, and the study did not continuously track movement.
  • Unmeasured differences remain possible. Statistical adjustments cannot eliminate every confounder, such as aspects of overall fitness or access to stairs.
  • Relative risk is not personal absolute risk. The same relative association can correspond to very different absolute risk differences across individuals.
  • Not every study agrees on every outcome. A separate 2021 UK Biobank analysis found a modest association with all-cause mortality but did not find a lower cardiovascular mortality rate; its authors cautioned about residual confounding.

Important safety distinction: preventing a first event versus recovering from one

The study was not a trial of unsupervised stair workouts for people recovering from a heart attack or living with aortic disease. Someone with known coronary disease, recent cardiac symptoms or procedures, an aneurysm, or limits on exertion should obtain an individualized plan from their clinician or cardiac rehabilitation team before increasing exercise intensity. The American Heart Association describes supervised cardiac rehabilitation as an important part of recovery after a heart attack. Guidance for significant aortic disease also cautions against abrupt, intense exertion and heavy straining.

Walking gently at an approved intensity may be more appropriate than a stair-climbing challenge for some people. Stop exertion and seek urgent medical assessment for concerning symptoms such as chest discomfort, fainting, or severe unusual breathlessness.

The MKP conclusion

Verdict: scientifically credible association, oversold headline. Climbing several flights of stairs daily was associated with lower future ASCVD risk in a very large study, but it does not replace walking, establish causation, or provide a universal prescription. The strongest message is that ordinary movement matters—and that existing heart or aortic conditions change what is safe.

Discussion questions

  1. How should public-health reporting distinguish a 20% lower relative risk from the absolute benefit an individual might experience?
  2. What randomized or wearable-device studies would help test whether deliberately adding stairs causes measurable benefit?
  3. How can health guidance encourage activity without excluding people whose medical conditions or disabilities make stairs difficult or unsafe?
