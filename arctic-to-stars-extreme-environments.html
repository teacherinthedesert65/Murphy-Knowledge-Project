From the Arctic to the Stars: Learning to Live in Extreme Environments

Humans adapt their surroundings—and learn to live within their limits.

Published October 24, 2026

Adaptation works in both directions

Living in an extreme environment requires more than a strong building. People need dependable energy, food, water, maintenance, healthcare and relationships. Engineering changes local conditions, while knowledge, routines and cooperation help people adapt to the place. Arctic communities offer lessons in enduring habitation; Antarctic research stations provide controlled opportunities to investigate particular challenges.

These places are also homes and living cultures. Inuit knowledge and community leadership are central to northern projects, rather than incidental to a technological experiment.

Demonstrated: growing food in the Arctic

Naurvik, meaning “the growing place,” began operating in Gjoa Haven, Nunavut, in 2019. The Canadian Space Agency describes a community-led hydroponic system in three repurposed shipping containers, with grow, utility and power pods. Local technicians tend the system. Its first harvest included butter lettuce and microgreens. The agency describes wind and solar supplying almost all its energy. This is evidence of controlled food production in a harsh environment, not proof of complete community food independence. Source: Canadian Space Agency.

Inuvik's community greenhouse, converted from a hockey arena, provides another model: seasonal gardening under the summer midnight sun. Seasonal greenhouses and year-round illuminated containers meet different needs and have different energy requirements. Source: Town of Inuvik.

Measured: an Antarctic greenhouse for space research

The 2020 paper Biomass Production of the EDEN ISS Space Greenhouse in Antarctica During the 2018 Experiment Phase reports edible and inedible biomass from an experimental greenhouse serving Antarctica's Neumayer III station. Crop-specific harvest measurements help researchers evaluate food output and the residues a future recycling system would need to handle. The experiment does not establish a fully closed food cycle. Read the open-access paper.

A separate study monitored microbes in the EDEN ISS greenhouse, illustrating why food safety and contamination control belong alongside crop yields in habitat design. Read the microbial-monitoring paper.

MKP proposal: a complementary food system

The following is a design concept inspired by James Murphy's discussion, not a description of an already demonstrated Arctic installation.

  • Staple crops: evaluate sources of calories, protein and fats, rather than relying on leafy vegetables alone.
  • Fresh greens: test lettuce, herbs and potentially purslane for nutrition, acceptability and resource requirements. Purslane's oxalate content and its limited calorie contribution matter when planning diets. University of Wisconsin Extension background.
  • Edible fungi: investigate cultivated mushrooms on suitable, prepared plant residues. Fungi consume existing organic energy; they do not replace the need for photosynthesis or another primary energy input.
  • Resource recovery: measure water recovery and nutrient losses; process residues safely before reuse. Not every waste stream is suitable for direct contact with food crops.
  • A shared garden: include an accessible place to tend plants and spend time, while testing rather than assuming its benefits to well-being.

Mushroom cultivation would need appropriate separation, ventilation and sanitation. Closing a material loop also requires accounting for salts, pathogens, contaminants and nutrients that leave the system.

The human side: sleep, isolation and teamwork

Preparing for Mars: human sleep and performance during a 13 month stay in Antarctica, published in SLEEP, followed 13 men at Concordia Station using repeated sleep and performance measurements. It illustrates how polar environments can help researchers examine human responses to isolation, unusual light cycles and station conditions. A small, selected station crew cannot represent every permanent community or future space population. Read the study.

Energy and maintenance determine what lasts

In a proposed settlement, wind and seasonal solar could be assessed alongside storage, backup generation and heat recovery. Geothermal energy depends on local geology and the required temperatures. Recovering generator or equipment heat could reduce heating demand, but cannot eliminate the energy needed for artificial lighting. Any design must survive its worst season and equipment failures, not just its average day.

The 2021 paper Assessing the Constraints to the Adoption of Containerized Agriculture in Northern Canada examines barriers to adoption and scaling. It is a useful starting point for asking whether equipment, financing, skills and local needs fit together. Read the open-access paper.

What carries over to space—and what does not

Polar work helps investigate logistics, maintenance, controlled agriculture and isolated teams. It does not reproduce lunar or Martian gravity, vacuum, or the radiation environment beyond Earth's protection. Even successful polar facilities depend on external supply chains. A productive greenhouse is one component of resilience; it is not evidence that a habitat can manufacture its own equipment or meet every nutritional need indefinitely.

Questions for investigation and discussion

  • What would a community need to produce and recycle locally before it could thrive with very little outside support?
  • How much edible energy and protein does each crop deliver per unit of electricity, water and growing area?
  • Which residues can safely support edible fungi, and which need other treatment?
  • What spare parts, specialist skills and emergency supplies remain essential?
  • How should residents shape the system around their food preferences, knowledge and priorities?
