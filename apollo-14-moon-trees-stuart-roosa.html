Apollo 14’s Moon Trees: Seeds That Orbited the Moon and Took Root on Earth

Stuart Roosa carried tree seeds around the Moon in 1971. The resulting trees became living links between space exploration and forests on Earth.

ADDED NOVEMBER 12, 2026 • HISTORICAL FEATURE

A forester’s connection to the Moon

During Apollo 14 in 1971, command module pilot Stuart Roosa carried hundreds of tree seeds in his personal kit. A former U.S. Forest Service smokejumper, Roosa stayed in lunar orbit aboard Kitty Hawk while Alan Shepard and Edgar Mitchell explored the surface.

The joint NASA–Forest Service project included loblolly pine, sycamore, sweetgum, redwood and Douglas fir. Seeds kept on Earth provided comparison material.

They traveled around the Moon: The seeds did not land on its surface, and the trees later grew in Earth’s soil. Roosa carried them; Mitchell was a fellow Apollo 14 crew member.

From damaged containers to living trees

The seed bags opened during post-flight decontamination, raising doubts about survival. Nevertheless, Forest Service germination efforts produced seedlings. Many were distributed for planting in 1975–1976, including U.S. bicentennial ceremonies; others went abroad.

NASA’s historical account reports no discernible difference between the flown trees and Earth-bound comparisons after decades. The story demonstrates successful survival and growth, rather than evidence of extraordinary lunar-induced abilities.

A history still being documented

Complete distribution records were not maintained. NASA’s Moon Tree list identifies known locations and marks trees no longer alive. A listed planting is therefore not automatically a surviving tree today.

MKP reflection

A tree can make space history tangible: something rooted in an ordinary landscape carries an extraordinary journey in its provenance. For a classroom investigation, compare the questions “Did the seeds survive?” and “Did spaceflight change their growth?” The second requires controls and measurements beyond the existence of a healthy tree.

Another useful project is historical documentation. What records would establish a tree’s identity—planting photographs, forestry correspondence, accession numbers, or a continuous chain from seed to seedling? Those records matter as much as a commemorative sign.
