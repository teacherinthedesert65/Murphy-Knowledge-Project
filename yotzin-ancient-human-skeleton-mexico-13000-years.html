ARCHAEOLOGY • FIRST AMERICANS • NOVEMBER 22, 2026

Yotzin: A 13,000-Year-Old Window into Ancient Mexico

Archaeologists excavating near Santa Lucía in the State of Mexico recovered an unusually robust human skeleton in 2021, among sediments also containing Late Pleistocene animal remains, including mammoths and prehistoric camels. Newly reported laboratory analyses indicate that the individual, named Yotzin, lived approximately 12,000 to 13,000 years before present. The discovery adds rare direct skeletal evidence to the story of early populations in the Basin of Mexico.

How old are the remains?

Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) announced two dating assessments. A Belgian laboratory's collagen-based analysis yielded an age range of 13,087–12,987 years before present. A University of Georgia laboratory's bioapatite-based analysis yielded 12,050–11,700 years before present. These are not identical results, and their differences deserve attention rather than being flattened into one precise birthday. The institute described the combined findings as placing Yotzin among the 23 oldest known human skeletal specimens in the Basin of Mexico.

The 2021 discovery

Archaeologist Israel Peña found the remains during archaeological salvage work associated with the Felipe Ángeles International Airport (AIFA). The bones came from carefully documented deposits associated with late Ice Age fauna. According to INAH, no accompanying grave offerings or artifacts were found. The controlled excavation and preserved contextual records are especially important for evaluating the skeleton's age and environment.

What the bones reveal

Researchers described a heavily built individual, about 1.75 meters tall, with pronounced dental wear and no cavities observed. Such observations can inform hypotheses about diet and habitual activities, but do not independently establish the individual's occupation, cause of death, or complete lifestyle. The dark coloration of the bones resembles that of some nearby Pleistocene fossils; color alone is not a reliable dating method.

What does Yotzin mean for the peopling of the Americas?

Yotzin provides rare evidence for people living in central Mexico near the Pleistocene–Holocene transition. It would be inaccurate to call Yotzin one of the very first people ever to enter the Americas. Evidence for human presence elsewhere in the Americas extends earlier, and the migration history remains an active field of research. A single skeleton cannot establish an entire population's origins.

What comes next?

INAH researchers reported plans for genomic analysis through the University of Burgos in Spain, with results potentially available in early 2027. Ancient DNA, if recoverable and interpretable, could help explore population relationships and migration history. Until those findings are published, claims about Yotzin's ancestry should remain tentative.
