Earth's Magnetic Weak Spot: Understanding the South Atlantic Anomaly

A changing magnetic field connects processes deep inside Earth with radiation hazards in orbit.

OCTOBER 9, 2026

What is the anomaly?

The South Atlantic Anomaly is a broad region of weaker magnetic field over South America and the South Atlantic. Trapped energetic particles reach lower altitudes there, increasing radiation exposure for satellites crossing the region. NASA reports no visible effects on daily life at Earth's surface.

A generator inside Earth

Motion in Earth's electrically conducting, iron-rich liquid outer core generates the magnetic field. This geodynamo produces a changing, uneven field. The anomaly reflects that complexity, including the geometry of the field and patterns of core motion.

What Swarm measured

In October 2025, ESA reported results from eleven years of Swarm satellite observations. Since 2014, the weak-field region had gained an area comparable to almost half of continental Europe. That comparison describes the added area, rather than the anomaly's total size.

Change is uneven: the region southwest of Africa weakened faster after 2020. Researchers connect this behavior with patches of reversed magnetic flux near the core's upper boundary, where the local field opposes the dominant pattern. Swarm also observes strengthening elsewhere, including Siberia. Regional weakening does not mean the entire field is changing identically.

The date matters

The Futura article shared with MKP was dated October 9, 2026. Its central Swarm finding was announced by ESA on October 13, 2025. This explainer distinguishes the date of the news coverage from the date of the underlying research announcement.

Why satellite operators care

Energetic particles can upset onboard computers, contaminate measurements or damage electronics. Operators may suspend sensitive instruments during crossings. Tracking the anomaly helps plan these precautions; shielding and operational choices also matter.

Should people on the ground worry?

The field is weaker locally, rather than absent. Surface conditions differ from orbital conditions, and the atmosphere provides substantial shielding. The anomaly's existence alone does not predict when a global magnetic reversal might occur. Monitoring supports spacecraft planning and research into Earth's interior.

Why this belongs in MKP

This topic joins Earth science, physics and space engineering. It invites a useful question: how can measurements made above our planet reveal changes thousands of kilometres below us?
