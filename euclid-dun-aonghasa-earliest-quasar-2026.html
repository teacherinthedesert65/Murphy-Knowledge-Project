Euclid Discovers the Most Distant Known Quasar

J1504+6252, nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, pushes the quasar record back by 40 million years.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 24, 2026

A window into cosmic dawn

Julien Wolf and colleagues report a quasar at redshift 8.13 in Astronomy & Astrophysics. Its light reveals an active galactic nucleus when the universe was approximately 630 million years old. This is an observation of its existence at that epoch, not a measurement of its exact formation date.

How the discovery was made

Euclid's broad infrared survey identified the source. Keck, the Large Binocular Telescope, Hubble and James Webb provided follow-up observations. Webb's measurements indicate a central black hole weighing approximately 360 million solar masses.

Why its feeding rate matters

The black hole appears to be accreting relatively slowly at the observed epoch, making its enormous early mass particularly intriguing. Its earlier growth history may have involved much faster accretion or a large initial seed. Direct collapse of a gas cloud is a plausible route, but the observations do not establish a unique formation mechanism.

Understanding redshift

Cosmic expansion stretches light wavelengths. A redshift of 8.13 corresponds to a wavelength stretching factor of 9.13. The light has traveled for roughly 13.1 billion years; this lookback time should not be confused with the object's present-day distance.

MKP perspective

The result constrains models of black-hole growth rather than invalidating the Big Bang. Comparing this quasar with more early active galaxies could help researchers determine whether large seeds, intermittent rapid accretion or other processes dominate. The exact mass and accretion history remain subject to measurement and modeling uncertainties.
