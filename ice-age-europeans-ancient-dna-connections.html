ANCIENT DNA • HUMAN ORIGINS • OCTOBER 23, 2026

Ice Age Europeans: Connected Across a Continent

New evidence links distant populations while tracing ancestry that persisted through cold millennia.

A woman’s genome connects east and west

A study published in Science Advances analyzed ancient human remains and cave-sediment DNA. A roughly 30,000-year-old woman from Isturitz in southwestern France had mainly western European ancestry, with a contribution related to central and eastern populations. Modeling places that genetic mixing around 32,500–31,500 years ago.

Continuity through the cold

Evidence from Chufín in northern Spain supports regional ancestry persistence. At El Mirón, researchers recovered human nuclear DNA from approximately 22,000-year-old sediment without human bones or teeth. This supports an Iberian refuge during the Last Glacial Maximum.

Family relationships in Italy

Two Ostuni individuals were probably mother and son. Their genetic data suggest the parents belonged to the same population but were not close relatives.

Keep the interpretation precise

Gene flow establishes ancestry connections; it does not map an individual’s journey or prove that everyone routinely traveled across Europe. Regional continuity can coexist with migration. One family cannot establish universal marriage rules or prove deliberate avoidance of inbreeding. “Gravettian” describes an archaeological tradition, not one uniform genetic population.

Questions for discussion

  • What can DNA tell us that similar tools alone cannot?
  • What additional evidence would distinguish direct travel from connections built over many generations?
  • How might sediment DNA change which places archaeologists investigate?
