Halloween Skywatching: Saturn, Stars and Taurid Meteors

A dated guide to the October 31–November 1, 2026 US night sky, with realistic expectations for moonlight and Taurid fireballs.

ADDED OCTOBER 31, 2026 • OBSERVING WINDOW: OCTOBER 31–NOVEMBER 1, 2026

This guide is specific to Halloween weekend 2026 in the United States. Weather, light pollution, latitude and the time you observe all affect what you can see.

Start with Saturn and the autumn stars

NASA’s October guide highlights Saturn following its October 4 opposition, when the planet was opposite the Sun in our sky. It remains a rewarding evening target. A telescope can reveal its rings; an unaided-eye view shows a bright point of light.

The Pleiades, or Seven Sisters, are another autumn target. NASA recommends looking east in the evening as the cluster climbs higher; binoculars reveal more stars. Its close encounter with the Moon occurred October 27–28, before Halloween weekend.

Taurids: occasional fireballs, modest rates

The Southern and Northern Taurid showers overlap in late October and early November. The American Meteor Society lists nominal peak nights of November 4–5 and November 11–12. Both are low-rate showers, around five meteors per hour each under ideal conditions, with the possibility of unusually bright meteors called fireballs. The Society does not identify 2026 as an enhanced Southern Taurid swarm year.

“Halloween fireballs” is a nickname, not a promise of a dramatic display on October 31. A meteor can appear anywhere across the sky; a wide unaided-eye view is more useful for this than a telescope.

Moonlight still matters

The Moon is waning after the October 26 full Moon. A waning gibbous is more than half illuminated, so calling it both “half-lit” and gibbous is imprecise. Its light can obscure faint meteors and deep-sky objects. Choose a dark, open location, let your eyes adjust, and check local Moon-rise times rather than assuming ideal darkness all night.

Make it an observation, not a prediction contest

MKP activity: Spend 20 minutes recording the start time, location, cloud cover, visible Moon, brightest objects and any meteors. Compare two observers’ logs. Which differences reflect the sky, and which reflect viewing conditions or attention?
