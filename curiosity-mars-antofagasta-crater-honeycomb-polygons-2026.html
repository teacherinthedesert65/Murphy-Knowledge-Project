Curiosity Finds Honeycomb-Shaped Ground Near a Mars Crater

Images from the Antofagasta crater area highlight thousands of striking polygonal shapes—and raise questions about how the Martian surface cracked and changed.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 23, 2026

What the report describes

A report from Futura Sciences describes NASA's Curiosity rover reaching a roughly 32-foot (about 10-meter) crater named Antofagasta in April 2026. The nearby surface is covered with extensive polygonal, honeycomb-like patterns. Such repeated geometric forms offer clues to the processes that shaped the rocks and soil.

Why polygons form on Mars

Polygonal patterns can arise when a surface fractures under stress. On Mars, scientists investigate multiple mechanisms: drying and shrinkage of once-wet sediment, thermal contraction, changes involving subsurface ice, and fractures that later become more visible as erosion removes softer material. The appearance of a polygon by itself does not identify which mechanism formed it.

What Curiosity can investigate

Curiosity carries cameras, a drill, spectrometers and instruments for analyzing rock chemistry and mineralogy. Images reveal the arrangement and scale of fractures; chemical and mineral evidence can help distinguish histories involving water, sediment deposition, alteration and erosion. The rover's broader exploration of Gale crater seeks to reconstruct past environments that could have supported microbial life.

Evidence check: The attached news screenshot identifies the Antofagasta site and the polygonal terrain. It does not, by itself, establish that these features were formed by water, ice or biological activity. Any specific formation mechanism requires supporting observations and geological analysis.

Why this matters

Repeated fracture patterns preserve a record of physical conditions long after those conditions have changed. Mapping their shapes, orientations and relationships to surrounding rock layers can help researchers reconstruct environmental history on Mars. A seemingly simple patch of cracked ground can therefore be a useful geological archive.

MKP perspective

The compelling part of this story is not that geometric shapes are automatically mysterious, but that their origin can be tested. The next questions are whether the polygons cut through individual layers, whether mineral deposits fill the fractures, and whether their distribution matches drying, cooling or erosion. Those details turn a dramatic photograph into a scientific explanation.
