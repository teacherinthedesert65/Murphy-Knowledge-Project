Homo erectus in China: Teeth Reveal a Longer, More Adaptable History

Ancient enamel proteins and cave dating extend the story into southern China’s rainforests, with evidence reaching approximately 184,000 years ago.

ADDED NOVEMBER 18, 2026

The new finding is younger than the screenshot suggests

Two Nature studies published November 18, 2026 describe teeth from Naxian and Baxian caves in Guangxi, southern China, dated to approximately 324,000–184,000 years ago. This pushes the securely identified regional record beyond the roughly 400,000-year benchmark discussed in earlier research.

One study combined 43 radiometric ages from five dating techniques with chemical evidence from teeth. Its environmental reconstruction supports use of closed rainforest habitats, challenging the familiar picture of Homo erectus confined to more open landscapes.

Nine teeth, with one unresolved identity

The identification study reports nine hominin teeth. Eight are attributed to H. erectus using morphology, with protein evidence supporting four of them among five teeth studied molecularly. Those four carry a distinctive ameloblastin protein substitution previously reported in Chinese H. erectus.

The fifth molecularly studied tooth, PA1796, differs from those specimens and remains uncertain in identity. The researchers discuss a possible Denisovan connection; it should not be presented as a confirmed Denisovan tooth.

Proteins are not a complete genome: This work recovers information from preserved proteins. It does not report whole ancient DNA genomes from all nine teeth. A useful molecular marker can help identify a fossil without reconstructing its entire ancestry.

Putting the extension in perspective: The difference between 400,000 and 184,000 years is about 216,000 years—roughly 8,640 intervals of 25 years. This arithmetic illustrates the scale; it is not evidence of an unbroken population lasting for every year between those dates. Nor is the youngest known fossil automatically the species’ extinction date.

What about interbreeding?

The earlier six-tooth study found two shared amino-acid variants in Chinese specimens approximately 400,000 years old. One variant also occurs in Denisovans. Its authors proposed that populations related to H. erectus may have contributed ancestry to Denisovans, with some such ancestry subsequently reaching modern humans.

That is a proposed route of ancient gene flow. It does not establish a direct mating event between the newly identified Guangxi individuals and Homo sapiens. Finding different lineages in the same broad region and period also does not, by itself, demonstrate that particular individuals met.

MKP reflection: endurance is more than a date

The significance is twofold: a later regional record and a broader picture of ecological adaptability. A fossil can change a timeline; evidence about its environment can change how we imagine the organism living.

Human evolution is especially vulnerable to a ladder-like story in which every earlier species appears less capable than the next. These findings invite a more careful question: which capabilities are demonstrated by the evidence, and which limitations have we merely assumed?

Discussion question: If you could add one discovery to this record—a skull, more enamel proteins, ancient DNA, or a securely dated living site—which would most improve our understanding, and why?
