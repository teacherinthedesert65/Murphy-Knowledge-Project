When Einstein's Relativity Changes Chemical Bonds

Experiments on carbon and bismuth reveal why the familiar textbook triple bond does not always describe molecules containing very heavy elements.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 23, 2026 • PEER-REVIEWED STUDY JULY 2026

The discovery

A team led by Brown University chemist Lai-Sheng Wang, with Deniz Kahraman and Jie Hui and collaborators including Kirk A. Peterson of Washington State University, reported direct spectroscopic evidence that relativistic effects reorganize bonding in the carbon-bismuth anion, CBi⁻. Their paper appeared in Science on July 9, 2026.

For many familiar molecules, a triple bond is described as one sigma (σ) bond formed along the line joining two atomic nuclei and two pi (π) bonds formed by sideways orbital overlap. In CBi⁻, however, the measurements and fully relativistic calculations indicate a different electronic structure: one π-like Kramers pair and two pairs containing substantial σ/π mixing. The result is sometimes summarized as one π bond and two hybrid sigma-pi bonds.

Why bismuth changes the rules

Bismuth has atomic number 83. Its large nuclear charge produces strong relativistic effects in its electrons. In particular, spin-orbit coupling links an electron's spin to its orbital angular momentum, so the two can no longer be treated as independent labels in the usual way. States that conventional chemistry classifies separately as σ and π can mix when they have the same projection of total angular momentum.

This is a consequence of relativistic quantum mechanics, not gravitational time dilation or curved spacetime. The research uses the four-component Dirac-Coulomb framework to describe the electronic structure.

How the researchers tested it

The team produced CBi⁻ ions using laser ablation of a bismuth-and-graphite target. They cooled the ions and measured the energies and angular distributions of electrons detached using light in high-resolution photoelectron spectroscopy. These measurements were compared with advanced relativistic coupled-cluster calculations. The agreement supports the conclusion that the traditional σ + 2π description breaks down for this molecule.

Evidence check: This is experimental evidence for a specific heavy-element molecular ion, supported by quantum-chemical calculations. It does not mean the familiar triple-bond model fails for ordinary light-element molecules, or that all heavy-element compounds must have the same bonding pattern.

Why this matters

The finding provides a clear test of predictions made over decades: relativity can affect not merely small numerical corrections but the qualitative way chemists classify chemical bonds. Better descriptions of heavy-element bonding could eventually improve research on organobismuth chemistry and materials involving heavy atoms. Potential applications in solar cells or quantum materials are longer-term possibilities, not technologies demonstrated by this experiment.

MKP analysis

This study is a powerful example of how physics and chemistry intersect. The standard sigma/pi model remains an excellent approximation in its domain, while relativistic quantum mechanics reveals a deeper description needed for sufficiently heavy atoms. Scientific progress here refines a successful model rather than making chemistry textbooks wholly obsolete.
