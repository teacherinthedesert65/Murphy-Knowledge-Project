Two-Million-Year-Old Jaw Reveals a Modern Dental Mystery

An ancient South African fossil reveals a familiar anatomical variation with an uncertain origin.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 23, 2026

The discovery

In a study published November 20 in Evolutionary Human Sciences, Luca Morris-Obst and colleagues described prominent bony growths on both sides of the inner lower jaw of Sts 52b, an Australopithecus africanus specimen from Sterkfontein. The deposits containing the fossil are dated to approximately 2.1–2.8 million years ago. The fossil was excavated in 1949 and the feature was previously noted in 1977; the new paper provides a detailed anatomical assessment.

What is a mandibular torus?

A mandibular torus is a benign extra growth of bone on the tongue-facing side of the lower jaw. Examination of the ancient specimen reveals a substantial ridge composed mainly of dense cortical bone, similar to the variation seen in living humans.

Why the individual's age matters

The second molars were already in place, but the wisdom teeth had not finished emerging. This indicates a young individual rather than a mature adult. A large ridge at this developmental stage challenges explanations requiring decades of chewing or grinding.

Comparison with other hominins

The researchers report no known examples in Paranthropus robustus, despite its powerful chewing apparatus. This contrast weakens the hypothesis that high biting forces alone necessarily produce tori. It does not rule out mechanical stress as a contributing factor.

What remains unknown

Inherited predisposition, developmental processes and environmental influences may interact. One fossil cannot establish the precise cause or its frequency in ancient populations. Earlier possible examples in Australopithecus afarensis are considerably less pronounced.

MKP perspective

The discovery connects evolutionary biology with everyday dentistry. Comparing jaw development across fossil and living populations could help researchers separate the effects of inheritance, age and mechanical loading. The remarkable continuity of anatomical variation is evidence of deep evolutionary history, not proof of a particular genetic mechanism.
