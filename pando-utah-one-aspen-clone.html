Pando: Thousands of Aspen Trunks, One Remarkable Clone

Utah’s Pando challenges what we mean by a tree: roughly 47,000 stems belong to a sprawling quaking-aspen clone.

ADDED NOVEMBER 14, 2026

A forest of one

Near Fish Lake in Utah’s Fishlake National Forest, Pando is a quaking-aspen clone (Populus tremuloides) covering approximately 106 acres, or 43 hectares. Its estimated 47,000 aboveground stems are called ramets: repeated growth units of the same genetic individual, or genet.

New shoots arise from the spreading root system. The trunks may look like separate trees, but they belong to a single clonal lineage. “Pando” means “I spread.”

How heavy is it?

A widely cited estimate puts Pando’s combined aboveground and belowground mass at about 13 million pounds—roughly 5.9 million kilograms. This is an estimate, not a direct weighing. Pando is celebrated as an exceptionally massive organism, but “largest” depends on whether the comparison concerns mass, area, or another measure.

The clone and its trunks have different lifetimes

Individual trunks die and are replaced while the clone can persist. Counting one trunk’s growth rings therefore does not date the entire organism. Claims about Pando’s exact age should be treated as estimates rather than a settled birthday.

Survival depends on replacement growth

In a 2018 study, Rogers and McAvoy monitored 65 plots and examined historical aerial images. They found that browsing, especially by mule deer, strongly affected successful regeneration. Protected areas showed better recruitment, emphasizing that producing shoots is not enough: those shoots must survive to replace the canopy.

That is a dated research finding, not a complete assessment of conditions today. Friends of Pando reports expanded protective care and improving regeneration in its more recent updates. Continued monitoring is needed to evaluate recovery across the clone.

MKP reflection

Pando makes individuality less obvious than it first appears. A familiar single trunk is one way a tree can grow; a long-lived clone can renew itself through many stems. Its conservation also illustrates why the youngest, least conspicuous growth can matter as much as the tallest trees.

Discussion question: If you were monitoring Pando’s future, would you learn more by counting mature trunks, young shoots, or the number of shoots that survive into taller stems?
