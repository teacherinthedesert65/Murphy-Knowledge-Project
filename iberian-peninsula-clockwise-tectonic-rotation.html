Why Spain and Portugal Are Slowly Rotating Clockwise

Satellite and earthquake data reveal how Africa–Eurasia convergence helps turn Iberia, with different forces acting around Gibraltar.

ADDED NOVEMBER 8, 2026 • RESEARCH ANNOUNCED DECEMBER 2025

A slow turn at a complicated boundary

Spain and Portugal occupy most of the Iberian Peninsula. Research led by Asier Madarieta-Txurruka at the University of the Basque Country supports a clockwise rotation of Iberia within the complex contact zone between Africa and Eurasia. The plates converge at roughly 4–6 millimeters per year.

Two different measurements: That convergence rate measures distance, not an angle of rotation. It should not be read as every location in Spain or Portugal moving the same distance in the same direction.

Why Gibraltar matters

The researchers combined satellite measurements of surface deformation with earthquake-derived stress information. Their interpretation distinguishes two regions: east of the Strait of Gibraltar, deformation in the Gibraltar Arc helps absorb the collision; west of the strait, more direct plate interaction transmits forces toward southwestern Iberia. They propose that this uneven forcing helps produce the clockwise turn.

What the findings can tell us

Stress and deformation maps guide investigations of active faults and their earthquake potential. However, the precise observations span only decades, while tectonic evolution unfolds over millions of years. The university announced the work on December 10, 2025; it is therefore earlier research receiving renewed attention, rather than a discovery first announced today.

A question worth exploring

Imagine placing several markers on a moving surface. What pattern would distinguish a rotation from a simple sideways shift? What additional observations would you need if the surface also stretched or bent? These questions help explain why scientists combine different kinds of measurements rather than relying on a single motion arrow.
