A Message Through a Wall, Carried by Gravity

An elevator counterweight transmitted a word to a gravity sensor—demonstrating a physical principle with striking practical limits.

ADDED NOVEMBER 18, 2026

Moving mass becomes a message

University of Queensland researchers used a 1.8-tonne elevator counterweight as a transmitter. Two positions represented binary 0 and 1. A gravimeter in an adjacent room recovered the word “Gravity” through a brick wall, at approximately 0.7 metres’ range and one bit per minute.

The team’s work, Unblockable communication with gravity, is reported in Science Advances. UQ’s account is dated November 19, 2026 in Australia; an author preprint was posted in November 2025.

What carried the information?

Moving the counterweight changed the local gravitational field. The receiver measured that change with a classical mass–spring gravimeter. This was a slowly varying gravitational-field demonstration, rather than a radio transmission or a detector receiving gravitational radiation from a distant cosmic event.

These calculations exclude extra framing, calibration and error correction. The preprint describes two additional seven-bit control sequences, giving 63 transmitted bits in total—63 minutes, before background measurements. That explains the roughly one-hour summary without treating the seven-letter payload as 60 bits.

“Unblockable” has a specific meaning

The authors distinguish shielding from interference. Ordinary material does not provide a gravitational equivalent of an electromagnetic screen. However, other moving masses could confuse reception, and vibration could disturb the instrument. A signal can pass through a barrier while still being too weak or noisy to decode reliably.

Three different claims: Passing a wall does not imply unlimited range, guaranteed reception or secrecy. Shielding resistance describes a property of the physical channel; reliability and privacy require additional evidence and design.

Demonstration versus projection

UQ discusses possible ranges around 500 metres and rates around 15 bits per minute under ambitious assumptions involving improved systems. Those figures are modelling, not the performance of the elevator experiment. The practical challenge is to move sufficient mass, detect a tiny change and distinguish it from background effects.

Even a hypothetical 15-bit-per-minute channel would take about 32 minutes to send 100 eight-bit characters, excluding overhead. A fifteenfold improvement can be scientifically useful while remaining slow for ordinary messaging.

MKP reflection: a new channel is not yet a new phone

The achievement is the conversion of a controlled physical change into recoverable information. That is the common thread connecting a flashing lamp, a radio transmitter and this moving counterweight. Their usefulness differs because range, energy, noise and information rate differ.

A proof of principle earns attention without needing a promise of imminent replacement technology. The experiment makes an abstract fact tangible: even a barrier that conceals an object does not remove its gravitational influence.

Discussion question: What short message might still be valuable if it took minutes to send? What performance measurement would you require before trusting such a system outside a controlled experiment?
