Gen Z Uses AI While Questioning Its Costs: What Two Surveys Show

Gallup and Milltown Partners find frequent AI use alongside reservations, but their populations, questions, and timelines differ.

ADDED NOVEMBER 15, 2026

Gallup: Use stayed steady as enthusiasm fell

Gallup’s April 2026 report, with the Walton Family Foundation and GSV Ventures, found that 51% of U.S. respondents aged 14–29 used generative AI at least weekly: 22% daily and 29% weekly. The web survey used the probability-based Gallup Panel and included 1,572 respondents, surveyed February 24–March 4.

Compared with 2025, excitement fell from 36% to 22%, hopefulness from 27% to 18%, and anger rose from 22% to 31%. Anxiety remained statistically steady at 42%; it did not show the same increase as anger.

Trust figures need their correct population

Among employed Gen Z respondents, 69% placed more trust in work completed without AI, compared with 28% for AI-assisted work and 3% for solely AI-produced work. These findings concern workers’ comparative trust, not a test of output accuracy or a measure covering every Gen Z respondent.

Milltown: Frequent use and conflicted feelings

Milltown Partners’ November report, based on October 2026 survey data, reports daily AI use among 46% of Gen Z respondents and feelings of guilt about use among 30%. It describes a survey of 6,429 adults across the United States and United Kingdom, including a Gen Z sample boost, alongside four Gen Z focus groups.

Its interpretation emphasizes ambivalence: people can use a tool frequently while worrying about its effects on independent thinking, employment, or society.

Do not turn two surveys into a single trend line

Gallup measures generative AI use among U.S. 14–29-year-olds; Milltown covers adults across two countries and asks about AI. Different timing, age coverage, wording, and sampling mean that 22% and 46% daily use cannot, on their own, establish a rise of 24 percentage points.

These are self-reported attitudes and behavior. They do not demonstrate that AI caused cognitive decline, job loss, or guilt in a particular person.

MKP reflection: Use is not endorsement

Adoption and approval answer different questions. A person may value help with a particular task while distrusting other outputs or worrying about broader consequences. Treating all use as enthusiasm misses that distinction.

For educators, a useful discussion can ask students what assistance they accept, how they verify it, and which parts of an assignment must remain their own reasoning. Those are practical questions these surveys raise, rather than interventions the surveys tested.

Discussion question: What evidence would show that an AI tool is helping someone learn, rather than only helping them finish?
