Additive Manufacturing: From Digital File to Physical Part

3D printing builds objects layer by layer, opening design possibilities that are difficult—or uneconomical—to achieve with traditional manufacturing alone.

Murphy Knowledge Project • Reviewed September 2026

Additive manufacturing starts with a digital model and creates a physical part by adding material where it is needed. That sounds simple, but useful parts depend on the interaction among process, material, geometry, machine calibration, and post-processing.

What makes manufacturing “additive”

NIST defines additive manufacturing as fabricating parts by building them up layer by layer rather than primarily cutting material away or shaping it in a mold. Different systems may extrude melted polymer, cure liquid resin, fuse powder, deposit wire, or use other methods.

Material extrusionCommon desktop printers melt and deposit thermoplastic filament along programmed paths.
Vat photopolymerizationLight selectively cures liquid resin to create fine features and smooth surfaces.
Powder-bed processesEnergy fuses selected regions of polymer or metal powder, enabling complex industrial parts.

Why engineers use it

Additive manufacturing can shorten prototyping cycles, make customization more economical, and create internal channels, lattices, and complex shapes that would be difficult to machine. It can also reduce some forms of scrap because material is placed selectively rather than removed from a larger block.

The print is not the whole process

A successful part may require orientation decisions, supports, slicing parameters, heat treatment, washing, curing, machining, inspection, or surface finishing. Strength can vary with build direction and process conditions, so a visually correct part is not necessarily a structurally reliable one.

Materials change everything

Polymers, metals, ceramics, composites, and biomaterials behave differently during printing and after fabrication. Designers must consider heat resistance, toughness, fatigue, chemical exposure, dimensional stability, and the specific loads a part will experience.

3D printing is most powerful when the design is created for the process. Simply printing a shape that was designed for another manufacturing method often leaves much of additive manufacturing’s potential unused.

Where it fits best

Rapid prototypes, low-volume production, customized medical devices, tooling, repair parts, lightweight aerospace structures, and geographically distributed manufacturing are all strong use cases. Traditional processes can still be faster or cheaper for very high-volume simple parts, which is why additive manufacturing is best viewed as another manufacturing tool rather than a universal replacement.
