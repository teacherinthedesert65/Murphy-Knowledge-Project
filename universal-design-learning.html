Universal Design for Learning: Designing Access from the Start

UDL asks educators to anticipate barriers before they become problems and to design flexible pathways toward the same meaningful learning goals.

Murphy Knowledge Project • Reviewed September 2026

Universal Design for Learning is a framework for designing learning environments that give students multiple ways to engage with learning, perceive and understand information, and demonstrate what they know.

The idea behind UDL

Students differ in language, background knowledge, sensory access, attention, communication, motor skills, and many other characteristics. UDL treats that variability as something instructional design should anticipate rather than as an exception that must always be fixed afterward.

EngagementOffer purposeful ways for learners to connect with goals, sustain effort, collaborate, and build agency.
RepresentationPresent important information in accessible forms and use multiple representations when they strengthen understanding.
Action & ExpressionProvide appropriate ways for learners to respond, communicate, navigate tools, and demonstrate learning.

Accessibility is not lowering the goal

A common misunderstanding is that more access means less rigor. Good UDL separates the learning goal from barriers that are irrelevant to that goal. If the target is scientific reasoning, for example, a student may not need handwriting to be the gatekeeper. If the target is fluent handwriting, however, handwriting itself is part of the goal.

UDL and explicit instruction can coexist

Flexible design does not mean leaving students to discover everything independently. Evidence-based intervention frequently relies on systematic teaching, carefully sequenced examples, clear language, guided practice, feedback, and deliberate review. The Institute of Education Sciences, for example, recommends systematic instruction and well-chosen representations for students struggling in elementary mathematics.

Technology can help—but only when it removes a real barrier

Text-to-speech, speech recognition, captions, adjustable text, alternative keyboards, visual supports, and other assistive or accessible technologies can widen participation. CAST also emphasizes that providing a tool is not enough; learners may need instruction and support to use it effectively.

The strongest version of UDL is not “give every student every option.” It is deliberate design: identify the learning goal, predict barriers, and provide flexibility where that flexibility improves access without obscuring what students are actually expected to learn.

What changed in UDL Guidelines 3.0

CAST released Guidelines 3.0 in 2024. The update retains the familiar framework while placing greater emphasis on learner agency, belonging, multiple perspectives, accessibility, and reducing barriers rooted in exclusionary systems and practices.
