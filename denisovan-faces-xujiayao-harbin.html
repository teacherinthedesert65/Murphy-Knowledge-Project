Putting a Face to Denisovans: Xujiayao, Harbin and Ancient Proteins

Fossil anatomy becomes more informative when molecular evidence identifies the lineage.

The new connection

A study published in Nature on October 21, 2026 links Xujiayao fossils from northern China to Denisovans through ancient protein evidence. Their preserved skull, jaw and tooth material expands the anatomical record available for comparison with the Harbin individual. [1]

Harbin supplies another anchor

The nearly complete Harbin cranium, widely nicknamed “Dragon Man,” was linked to Denisovans in 2025 through protein analysis and mitochondrial DNA recovered from dental calculus. This provides a molecularly identified skull against which other fossils can be compared. [2, 3]

What does “appearance” mean?

These discoveries bring skull and facial anatomy into clearer focus. They do not amount to a photograph of a living Denisovan. Facial reconstructions add interpretations of soft tissues, hair and other details that bone alone cannot establish.

Read the screenshot cautiously

The phrase “physically large” can blur different claims. A robust skull does not by itself establish height, body mass or muscularity across an entire lineage. Likewise, shared anatomical traits support comparisons, but fossil resemblance and direct molecular identification are different kinds of evidence. Readers should distinguish the sampled individuals from generalizations about all Denisovans.

Why it matters

This is a strong example of anatomy and molecular research working together: chemistry can help identify a fossil, and its bones can then reveal traits previously missing from the lineage’s known record.
