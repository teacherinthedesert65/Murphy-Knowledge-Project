Why Voyager Did Not Melt in 30,000–50,000-Kelvin Plasma

Voyager’s interstellar measurements illustrate a crucial distinction: hot particles in extremely thin plasma do not heat a spacecraft like a furnace.

ADDED NOVEMBER 10, 2026 • EXPLAINING EARLIER VOYAGER FINDINGS

What was actually measured?

A 2019 Nature Astronomy paper reports Voyager 2 measurements of 30,000–50,000 kelvin in the very local interstellar medium near the heliopause. That was hotter than the 15,000–30,000 K range anticipated by the models and observations discussed in the paper.

The heliopause separates solar-wind plasma from the surrounding interstellar medium. Voyager 1 crossed in 2012; Voyager 2 crossed on November 5, 2018. These were different crossings at different locations and times.

Temperature is not the same as heating rate

Plasma temperature describes the particles’ random kinetic motion. Spacecraft heating depends on how much energy reaches and is absorbed by the vehicle per unit time. Density, particle speed, interactions with the surface and radiation all matter.

In extremely sparse plasma, individual particles can carry substantial energy while relatively few strike the spacecraft. A high particle temperature therefore does not mean the spacecraft itself reaches that temperature. A furnace contains vastly more material able to transfer energy to an object.

No literal wall of fire: This is a plasma boundary, not a solid barrier or flame. The numerical temperature alone cannot determine whether a spacecraft will melt; its full energy balance determines its temperature.

Why the two probes need careful distinction

Voyager 2 had a working plasma science instrument during its crossing. Voyager 1’s equivalent instrument stopped working in 1980, so other instruments provided evidence of its transition. The quoted temperature range should not be presented as an identical direct measurement made independently by both probes.

MKP reflection

This is a useful example of why a dramatic number needs a physical context. Before comparing two temperatures, ask what was measured, how dense the material is, and how energy transfers to the object of interest. Which additional measurement would you request before estimating a spacecraft’s heating?
