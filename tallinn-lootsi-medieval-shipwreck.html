Tallinn’s Lootsi Shipwreck: Medieval Life Preserved

Shoes, tools and two rats turn a buried vessel into evidence of everyday life at sea.

A medieval ship beneath a modern building site

The Estonian Maritime Museum records the discovery at 8 Lootsi Street in Tallinn on March 31, 2022, during office-building construction. This is an older discovery receiving renewed attention, rather than a ship first uncovered in October 2026.

The museum’s 2022 account describes a surviving wreck approximately 24.5 metres long and 9 metres wide. It was divided into four sections and transported to the museum’s Seaplane Harbour site in July 2022. The museum calls it the Lootsi cog; its early description identifies cogs as single-masted vessels used in medieval Baltic and North Sea shipping. [1]

What survived inside?

The museum reports tools, leather shoes and weapons, alongside the remains of two ship rats. A compass is another remarkable find: the museum describes it as a candidate for Europe’s oldest surviving dry compass. That superlative is the museum’s assessment, rather than a certainty established here. [2]

These objects make the wreck valuable beyond its hull. Questions about footwear, maintenance, navigation and animal passengers bring ordinary shipboard life into view. The rats are archaeological remains—not animals that survived for centuries.

Dating the wood and preserving the evidence

Dendrochronological analysis—dating through tree-ring patterns—indicates the ship’s timber was felled around 1360. A timber-felling estimate should not be treated as an exact sinking date. [2]

The museum describes cleaning sand from the hull, documenting its components and taking scientific samples. Its conservation preparations include monitoring humidity to prevent rapid drying and mould. Excavation is therefore the beginning of a preservation project, rather than the end of the discovery. [2]

Why it belongs in MKP

This find links engineering history with materials science and archaeology. A useful way to read the evidence is to separate the physical finds from the stories they might support: a shoe is direct evidence of an object, while identifying its wearer or reconstructing a final voyage requires more information.
