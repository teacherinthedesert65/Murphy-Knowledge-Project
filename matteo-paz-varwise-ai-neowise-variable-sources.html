Matteo Paz and VarWISE: Finding a Changing Sky in Old Data

A telescope archive can hold discoveries long after the observations end. The challenge is learning which patterns deserve attention.

ADDED NOVEMBER 21, 2026 • RESEARCH STORY 2023–2026

A student, a mentor, and a very large archive

Matteo (Matthew) Paz began working with Caltech/IPAC astronomer Davy Kirkpatrick through the Summer Research Connection in 2023. A proposed examination of a small patch of sky grew into machine-learning research on NASA’s NEOWISE infrared observations. The work continued beyond the six-week placement; it was not a finished all-sky discovery made in a single summer.

Caltech’s April 2025 report described algorithms flagging roughly 1.5 million potential new objects and noted Paz’s single-author methods paper in The Astronomical Journal. Mentoring and specialist advice were part of the achievement, alongside his programming and mathematics. The story is more informative than the shorthand of a teenager working entirely alone with “homemade AI.”

What changes in a light curve?

A light curve records brightness over time. Repeated infrared measurements can reveal recurring changes, eclipses, or other variability that a single image cannot show. Caltech describes NEOWISE’s archive as approaching 200 billion detection rows: repeated measurements are not the same thing as 200 billion distinct astronomical objects.

Paz’s methods paper presents VARnet, combining Fourier and wavelet-based features with deep learning to find variability candidates. These signal-processing tools help represent patterns over time. This is a specialized astronomical analysis system, rather than a conversational AI simply looking at photographs.

The catalog is now available

The June 2026 multi-author VarWISE publication and NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive documentation provide a more precise view than the original headline. The pipeline groups detections and uses VARnet for variability detection and XGBoost for classification. Catalog entries include predicted object types and, where appropriate, estimated periods.

The Extended percentage corresponds to about 1.57 million sources, calculated from the published totals. The Pure selection is a more conservative catalog, not a separate population to add to the Extended count.

What “new” means: Finding previously unrecognized variability or assigning a new classification does not necessarily mean the source had never appeared in an image or another catalog. Predicted types also require appropriate scrutiny; a machine-learning label is not independent confirmation of an object’s physical nature.

Why confidence levels matter

A broad search can retain more interesting candidates while also admitting more questionable cases. A conservative selection trades some reach for stronger confidence. Neither choice is universally best: the right catalog depends on the research question and the cost of follow-up. The published work explicitly discusses caveats for different variable types.

MKP reflection

Scientific discovery can come from asking a new question of existing measurements. Access to data matters, but so do mathematical preparation, mentorship, persistence, and a way for others to inspect the results. This makes the project a useful classroom example of AI supporting inquiry: it produces evidence to investigate, not a substitute for scientific judgment.

Discussion: Would you begin a follow-up project with the larger catalog or the highest-confidence selection—and how would your goal affect that choice?
