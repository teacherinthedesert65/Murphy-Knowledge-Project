Mars’s “Spiders”: How Carbon Dioxide Ice Can Sculpt a Landscape

Laboratory experiments test the physics behind Mars’s branching polar terrain—and show why reproducing a process is different from rebuilding a whole landscape.

ADDED NOVEMBER 17, 2026

Geology with a memorable nickname

Mars’s “spiders” are branching troughs called araneiform terrain, rather than living creatures. Some extend more than a kilometer across. For scale, one kilometer is ten 100-meter sprints placed end to end; these are landscape-sized features, not small marks in the dust.

The leading explanation: ice, pressure, escape

In the Kieffer model, winter builds a layer of carbon dioxide ice. Spring sunlight passes through translucent ice and warms darker ground underneath. Ice at the base sublimates—changing directly from solid to gas. Pressure builds until gas escapes through openings, carrying dust and potentially scouring branching channels beneath the ice.

Think of the contrast with familiar melting snow: here the key transition skips liquid water. The proposed sculpting agent is escaping gas beneath dry ice, not a river or a biological web.

What NASA’s team reproduced

Lauren Mc Keown and colleagues reported experiments in September 2024 using JPL’s DUSTIE chamber. They condensed carbon dioxide ice onto simulated Martian soil, then warmed the sample from below. Gas-and-dust plumes and spider-like features emerged. Ice also formed between soil grains and fractured the material, suggesting an additional route to cracked patterns.

The table is a comparison of conditions, not a claim that the lab produced a kilometer-wide spider. A successful analogue tests a mechanism while preserving clear limits on size, heating, and environmental complexity.

Why “first” needs context

Earlier experiments, reported by the Open University in 2021, already produced branching patterns by placing prepared dry-ice blocks on granular material under Mars-like pressure. The 2024 work advanced the test by growing the ice in the chamber and driving the deposition-to-heating-to-plume sequence. Its novelty was more specific than the first-ever creation of any spider-like pattern.

Dates and evidence: This November 2026 feature revisits research published in 2024. The experiments support the proposed physics; they do not establish that every Martian formation developed identically or settle how fast the large channels grow.

MKP reflection

The scientific value lies in moving from resemblance toward a test. An orbital image gives us a shape. A model proposes a cause. An experiment asks whether that cause can produce the expected behavior, and unexpected results improve the next model. Similar-looking patterns alone are not enough to identify their origin.

Discussion question

Which difference would you test next: sunlight instead of a buried heater, soil grain size, ice thickness, or repeated seasonal cycles? What observation would count against your preferred explanation?
