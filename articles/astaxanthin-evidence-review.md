# Astaxanthin: The Red Antioxidant — What Does the Evidence Say?

*Murphy Knowledge Project | Health & Longevity | October 20, 2026*

Astaxanthin (pronounced **as-tuh-ZAN-thin**) is a reddish carotenoid made by certain microalgae and accumulated by salmon, shrimp, and other marine animals. Supplement marketers promote it for healthy aging, inflammation, exercise performance, skin, and cardiovascular health. How strong is the evidence?

## The video that sparked this investigation

In his YouTube video **“I Megadosed Astaxanthin for a Year — Here Are My Results,”** longevity creator **Siim Land** describes a personal experiment with this red carotenoid. It's an engaging entry point into a bigger question: **What can controlled human research actually tell us about astaxanthin?**

A one-person experiment can generate questions, but it cannot establish causation: changes may reflect diet, training, other supplements, measurement variability, or chance. The following review examines the published evidence separately from the creator's personal account.

## What researchers have found

A systematic review and meta-analysis of **14 studies** (literature searched through March 17, 2020) reported a modest association between astaxanthin supplementation and higher HDL cholesterol. Its analysis found lower C-reactive protein (CRP), a marker of inflammation, in subgroups taking astaxanthin for **at least 12 weeks** or at doses **above 12 mg/day**. The analysis did **not** find significant overall effects on several other measured outcomes, including blood pressure, BMI, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, fasting blood sugar, or HbA1c.

These findings are **not** evidence that astaxanthin prevents heart attacks, reverses arterial plaque, repairs aneurysms, or extends human lifespan. Subgroup findings, especially from small studies, can be unstable and need confirmation.

## Why it is biologically interesting

Astaxanthin can participate in antioxidant processes in laboratory models, but antioxidant activity in a test tube does not automatically translate into better health outcomes in humans. The body’s inflammatory and oxidative pathways are complex; lowering one blood marker does not prove a meaningful clinical benefit.

## What remains uncertain

- Whether changes in HDL or CRP translate into fewer cardiovascular events.
- Whether benefits for skin, vision, cognition, or athletic recovery are clinically meaningful and reproducible.
- Which dose, formulation, or treatment duration is best.
- Long-term safety, particularly with higher-dose supplementation and multiple medications.

## Safety and practical perspective

Food sources are different from concentrated supplements. Product strength and quality can vary. People taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications, or managing cardiovascular disease, should discuss supplements with their clinician or pharmacist before use; direct evidence on interactions is limited, and absence of evidence is not proof of safety. Avoid extrapolating from a YouTube self-experiment to general medical advice.

**MKP assessment:** Worth investigating, but not a proven longevity intervention. The most useful next studies would be larger, longer randomized trials that track meaningful health outcomes, not only biomarkers.
