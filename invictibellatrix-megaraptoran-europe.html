Invictibellatrix: A Century-Old Fossil Rewrites the Story of Megaraptorans in Europe

A fragment of a dinosaur skull, collected on England's Isle of Wight and preserved in a museum since 1924, has become important new evidence for the evolution and prehistoric distribution of large-clawed predatory dinosaurs.

MKP SCIENCE REVIEW • OCTOBER 14, 2026

Discovery at a glance

Scientific name: Invictibellatrix amberina. Group: Megaraptora, a lineage of predatory theropod dinosaurs. Age: about 120 million years ago, Early Cretaceous. Location: Wessex Formation, Isle of Wight, southern England. Specimen: part of the back of the skull (braincase), not a full skeleton. Significance: researchers identify it as the first known European megaraptoran, helping fill a conspicuous geographical gap.

What was in the drawer?

Amateur fossil hunter Reginald Walter Tooley recovered the specimen on the southwest coast of the Isle of Wight. It was among fossils acquired by London's Natural History Museum in 1924. For roughly a century, its scientific identity remained unrecognized. In 2026, Cassius D. Morrison, Charlie R. Scherer, and colleagues described it as a new genus and species in Royal Society Open Science.

That history matters: a fossil does not stop being scientifically useful when it enters a museum. Better comparative collections, imaging techniques and new evolutionary questions can reveal discoveries in material already excavated.

How can a partial skull identify a dinosaur?

The team used computed tomography (CT) to build a three-dimensional record of the fossil's internal and external structures. They compared details of its braincase with those of other two-legged carnivorous dinosaurs, including tyrannosaurs and spinosaurs. Distinctive anatomical combinations—among them an oval opening at the junction with the spinal cord and a characteristic hourglass-shaped crest—helped support recognition of a new species.

The researchers then examined the specimen's traits within computational evolutionary analyses. Across their tests, it fell among megaraptorans, a relatively poorly understood group of theropods. That identification is based on real, preserved bone anatomy, even though the animal's complete skeleton is unknown.

What might Invictibellatrix have looked like?

Based on comparisons with relatives, the researchers suggest an animal around 3–4 metres (10–13 feet) long, standing a little over 1.5 metres (5 feet) high. Megaraptorans are known for their grasping forelimbs and large curved hand claws; for this species, claws around 10–20 centimetres (4–8 inches) have been proposed. Feathers are also considered plausible.

Evidence check: the studied specimen is a skull fragment. The exact body size, limb proportions, feathering, colors and hunting behavior of this particular individual were not directly observed from a preserved complete body. Those details are reasoned reconstructions from related animals and their environments.

Why Europe changes the evolutionary story

Before this report, known megaraptoran fossils were largely absent from Europe, even though proposed dispersal models suggested these predators might have passed through Eurasia before spreading into southern landmasses. The Isle of Wight specimen supplies direct fossil evidence that at least one early megaraptoran lived in Europe.

The researchers combined the new identification with other fossil occurrences, evolutionary relationships and reconstructions of ancient geography to examine where megaraptorans may have originated and traveled. Their results are compatible with dispersal from Asia through Europe and eventually into regions that formed parts of Gondwana. Later in the Cretaceous, some southern megaraptorans reached much larger sizes and may have occupied top-predator roles.

What is established? This animal's presence in Europe, provided the anatomical identification holds up. What remains a hypothesis? The exact routes, timing, reasons for expansion, and any proposed disappearance from northern ecosystems. Fossils are incomplete samples of prehistoric life; an absence from a region's fossil record is not certain proof an animal was absent there.

Did it hunt humans—or was it a miniature T. rex?

Neither headline should be taken literally. Humans did not coexist with Invictibellatrix. Researchers suggested that a human-sized animal would be within the conceivable prey range for a predator of this scale, but this is a hypothetical illustration, not evidence of actual hunting behavior. Megaraptorans also differed from the familiar Tyrannosaurus rex body plan: they are reconstructed with relatively slender skulls and powerful arms equipped with long claws. Precisely how megaraptorans relate to tyrannosaurs within the theropod evolutionary tree has been an active area of research.

A dinosaur with a meaningful name

Invictibellatrix means approximately “unconquered female warrior.” The authors also explained that the name was intended as a message of resilience to people experiencing discrimination and oppression. The species name amberina refers in part to amber associated with the geological setting.

The MKP assessment

Verdict: a substantive, peer-reviewed paleontological discovery. Its greatest value is not the striking speculation about claws or possible prey. It is the combination of a diagnostic historical fossil, modern CT-based anatomy, and a testable new biogeographic puzzle. The discovery illustrates how the scientific value of museum collections grows as methods and questions improve.

Questions for further investigation

  3. Which features in a scientific illustration are preserved evidence, and which are inferred from evolutionary relatives?
  4. What other historical museum fossils might yield new species when examined with CT scanning?
