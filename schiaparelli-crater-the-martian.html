Schiaparelli Crater: The Real Mars Behind The Martian

An enormous crater, ancient river valleys and a fictional astronaut's race for survival meet in the latest high-resolution look at the Red Planet.

MKP DEEP DIVE • OCTOBER 13, 2026

From science fiction to a real landscape

In Andy Weir's The Martian, astronaut Mark Watney's journey leads to a planned Ares 4 ascent vehicle in Schiaparelli Crater. The site is not just an invented backdrop. Schiaparelli is a real impact basin on Mars, and scientists have studied its complex geological history for decades. NASA has even examined the novel's proposed Ares 4 site with the HiRISE camera aboard Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.

On August 19, 2026, the European Space Agency (ESA) and German Aerospace Center (DLR) released a new virtual flyover of Schiaparelli based on imagery and elevation measurements from the Mars Express High Resolution Stereo Camera. The result reveals a landscape shaped not by one event but by billions of years of impacts, volcanism, sediment deposition, wind and, most intriguingly, ancient water.

Fast facts

Diameter: approximately 450–460 km (280–286 miles), depending on the source and measurement convention. Region: the ancient southern highlands of Mars, near Noachis Terra. Namesake: Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli (1835–1910). Latest flyover: released August 19, 2026, from Mars Express HRSC data.

Why is this gigantic impact crater so shallow?

Schiaparelli measures nearly half a thousand kilometres across, yet its present basin relief is much less than a fresh impact scar of similar size might suggest. DLR estimates the original depression may have been roughly four to five kilometres deep. Today, the rim rises only around two kilometres above the infilled basin.

The missing depth is a history book. Over billions of years, volcanic material, windblown sand and dust, and sediments possibly carried by rivers or accumulated in lakes have partially filled the crater. Some younger craters inside the larger basin are themselves nearly buried. Wrinkle ridges on the floor point to deformation associated with broad volcanic plains; dark volcanic sand has also collected along parts of the rim. A single Mars image can therefore record multiple eras of planetary change.

Evros Vallis: a fossil waterway

South of Schiaparelli, the branching valley system called Evros Vallis runs for approximately 350 kilometres across Noachis Terra. Its channels are evidence of water-related erosion in Mars's distant past, more than 3.5 billion years ago. DLR interprets the regional landscape as evidence that early Mars once experienced aspects of an active hydrological cycle: precipitation, surface runoff, rivers, lakes and evaporation.

Transported sediment from Evros Vallis may have helped fill parts of Schiaparelli. But a river-shaped valley does not tell scientists exactly how long a river flowed, how continuously the climate supported surface water, or whether a particular body of water contained life. Those questions require examination of landforms, rock chemistry, sediment layers and the timing of geological events.

What about a lake inside the crater?

ESA's earlier Mars Express investigations identified features consistent with ancient water-related sediment deposition within Schiaparelli, including dark deposits and landforms resembling material left by evaporating lakes on Earth. These observations make a former lake environment plausible, but they do not establish a single, permanent lake filling the whole basin for millions of years. Much of the visible terrain has been reworked since its formation.

For astrobiology, potential lake deposits matter because quiet water can sort and preserve sediment. On Earth, some sedimentary environments protect chemical evidence of old environments and sometimes traces of life. On Mars, finding a landscape that could have preserved such evidence is an invitation to investigate—not a discovery of biology.

How Mars Express makes a virtual flight possible

Launched in 2003, ESA's Mars Express has collected stereo images of Martian terrain over many years. The High Resolution Stereo Camera, operated by DLR, produces overlapping views from which researchers build digital terrain models. By combining those elevation models with image mosaics, teams can render a three-dimensional route across a landscape that no human has visited.

The 2026 animation begins near Evros Vallis, crosses ancient valley networks and the crater rim, and ends near the Brazos Valles channels. Its steepened relief helps viewers grasp topography but is not a one-to-one presentation of Martian slopes. This is an important distinction whenever beautiful planetary imagery is used as scientific evidence.

The Martian: what the story gets right—and wrong

Weir chose real locations and real engineering constraints to anchor a fictional survival story. The proposed Ares 4 destination in the novel is a smaller, dust-covered crater on Schiaparelli's southwestern floor. NASA's orbital imagery confirms that the area is unusually smooth and extensively mantled by dust—interesting visually, but problematic for field geology and for managing temperatures and surface equipment.

One famous exception to the novel and film's realism is the opening dust storm. Martian dust storms are genuine and can darken the skies or interfere with solar power. But Mars's atmosphere is so thin that even high wind speeds exert far less force than similarly fast winds on Earth. NASA says the story's equipment-wrecking storm would not realistically have the force depicted. The real long-term hazards include dust accumulation, cold, radiation, limited power and the immense difficulty of sustaining life far from Earth.

Could an ancient Schiaparelli have supported life?

It is scientifically reasonable to ask whether persistent water, mineral nutrients and energy sources once made portions of this region habitable for microbes. It is not scientifically justified to assert that life existed here. Habitability describes a possible environment; evidence of life requires much stronger, independent chemical, geological and potentially biological clues.

That distinction is central to the Murphy Knowledge Project: satellite imagery lets us identify promising questions, but not skip the work needed to answer them.

The MKP perspective

Schiaparelli links exploration, storytelling and planetary history in one location. The same crater that provides a dramatic destination in The Martian also preserves clues to a wetter, volcanically active Mars. Its real importance is not that science fiction happened there—it is that the landscape helps us test how a once-changing planet became the cold, dry world we see today.

Questions worth investigating next

How much of Schiaparelli's fill came from lava versus wind and water? Did its possible lakes form once or repeatedly? What do the nearby valley networks tell us about ancient climate? And which future orbital instruments, landers or sample-return efforts could separate these competing explanations?
