Crowland’s Hidden Henge: A Sacred Landscape Across Millennia

A search for a saint’s retreat uncovered a much older monument—and a story of people repeatedly giving meaning to the same place.

ADDED NOVEMBER 20, 2026 • EXCAVATIONS 2021–2022 • STUDY PUBLISHED 2024

Looking for one past, finding several

At Anchor Church Field near Crowland, Lincolnshire, archaeologists Duncan W. Wright and Hugh Willmott investigated a place traditionally associated with the hermits Guthlac and Pega. Their excavations revealed a monumental prehistoric henge, probably later adapted with a timber circle, followed by periods of abandonment and renewed occupation. The results appeared in the Journal of Field Archaeology in March 2024; this is older research being explored anew, rather than a discovery announced in November 2026.

What the ground revealed

The henge dates to the Neolithic or Early Bronze Age and ranks among the largest identified in the Fens of eastern England. It is an earthwork monument; the term does not mean that archaeologists discovered another Stonehenge-like arrangement of standing stones. The researchers trace renewed occupation of its interior to around the seventh century AD and a substantial hall-and-chapel complex to the later twelfth century.

Where Guthlac enters the story

Guthlac died in AD 714. His early biography describes a retreat in a previously disturbed mound, motivating efforts to locate his dwelling. The excavation’s Anglo-Saxon finds include pottery, two bone combs, and fragments of a fine glass drinking vessel. Later activity had destroyed the buildings of that period, preventing a secure connection to Guthlac.

Newcastle University’s account describes medieval earthworks still visible to later inhabitants and a prominent location beside the surrounding wetlands. The later hall may have accommodated elite visitors; documents suggest the chapel honored Pega, Guthlac’s sister. These connections help explain the site’s religious significance without establishing the saints’ exact residences.

Evidence versus tradition: The authors explicitly report no conclusive identification of an early hermitage. A saint’s biography, a local place-name, an artifact, and a building foundation provide different kinds of evidence. They can illuminate one another without all confirming the same claim.

A place can endure while its meanings change

The study interprets Crowland as an evolving sacred landscape, with later monastic investment helping connect the abbey to revered predecessors. That is different from demonstrating continuous worship or unchanged beliefs from prehistory into Christianity. Abandonment and rebuilding are part of the story, not inconvenient gaps to erase.

MKP reflection

An old monument can outlast the community that built it. Later people may preserve it, avoid it, reuse it, or invent a new explanation for it. Archaeology asks both what physically survived and how people responded to that survival. This makes Crowland a useful example of historical memory as something actively reshaped.

Discussion: What evidence would you need to distinguish a place genuinely occupied by a historical figure from a place later dedicated to that person?
