ESCAPADE’s Twin Spacecraft Complete Earth Flybys Toward Mars

Both powered flybys succeeded. NASA’s twin explorers are now heading toward Mars after a year-long detour near Earth.

ADDED NOVEMBER 9, 2026 • BOTH FLYBYS CONFIRMED COMPLETE

Two successful departures

NASA confirmed on November 9 that both ESCAPADE spacecraft completed their powered Earth flybys. The first passed closest to Earth around 5 a.m. EST on November 7; the second followed around 6 a.m. EST on November 9. Each combined a close Earth passage with an approximately nine-minute engine firing.

ESCAPADE stands for Escape and Plasma Acceleration and Dynamics Explorers. Its twins are now on course for a roughly ten-month cruise, with Mars orbit insertion burns planned for September 7 and 9, 2027. At Mars, they will investigate how the solar wind interacts with the planet and drives atmospheric loss.

Why take a year-long detour?

Launched November 13, 2025, the spacecraft initially followed a large, kidney-bean-shaped trajectory around the Earth–Sun L2 region. That route brought them back to Earth when the planetary geometry suited their departure for Mars.

Conventional efficient direct-launch opportunities recur about every 26 months. ESCAPADE’s alternative offers greater launch-date flexibility, while still timing the Mars-bound departure to favorable alignment. During the detour, the twins also sampled a distant part of Earth’s magnetotail; NASA’s pre-flyby update said scientists were preparing to share those observations.

The powered flyby and the Oberth effect

NASA calls this an Oberth maneuver: Earth’s gravity and an engine burn work together to redirect the spacecraft onto their Mars-bound paths. Its November 2 preview estimated that the burn would add more than 1,000 miles per hour to each spacecraft’s speed. This is a fuel-using maneuver, rather than a gravity-only flyby.

Status matters: Successful departure does not mean arrival has already happened. The cruise and the planned Mars orbit insertion burns remain ahead.

MKP reflection

The mission highlights a practical engineering tradeoff: a flexible launch date can be worth a longer journey. When comparing routes, what should count as “efficient”—fuel use, elapsed time, access to a launch vehicle, or total mission cost? The best answer depends on the mission’s constraints.
