AI Helps Amateur Astronomers Search for Hidden Exoplanets

Public telescope archives and AI-assisted programming are opening new avenues for independent scientific investigation.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 24, 2026

What researchers found

OpenAI reported that Kevin Bond used Codex to examine Kepler observations of approximately 100,000 stars, recovering known planets and identifying two promising signals independently reproduced by a SETI Institute astronomer. Ethan Byrd used Astra to examine observations covering about 19 million stars, identifying 49 initial candidates. These are potential planets, not confirmed discoveries.

How transit detection works

When a planet crosses the visible disk of its star, it can cause a small decrease in observed brightness. A graph of brightness against time is called a light curve. Repeated dips provide an estimate of orbital period. For a simple transit, the fractional loss of light is approximately the square of the ratio of planetary radius to stellar radius. Real observations require corrections for noise, stellar variability and other effects.

Independent efforts

Nature reported on November 20 that Pavel Rabtsevich used Claude Code to examine TESS observations of more than 120,000 stars. Two periodic signals from TIC 4206066 received independent analysis by mission astronomers and were listed as candidates. The initial report was a preprint, not a peer-reviewed confirmation.

Why verification matters

Eclipsing binary stars, instrument artifacts and stellar variability can resemble planetary transits. Finding a recurring signal is only the beginning. Additional observations, statistical validation and sometimes radial-velocity measurements are necessary to establish its origin. AI-generated code must also be tested for reproducibility and bias.

MKP perspective

This is an opportunity for students and citizen scientists to learn astronomy, programming and statistics through real observations. Open data can make scientific participation more accessible, but the standard of evidence must remain the same regardless of who writes the software. NASA's public archive provides a starting point for independent exploration.
