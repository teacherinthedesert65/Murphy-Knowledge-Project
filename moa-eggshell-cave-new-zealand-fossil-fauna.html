Moa Eggshell Cave: A Million-Year-Old Window Into New Zealand’s Wildlife

Fossils near Waitomo reveal 12 bird taxa and four frog species, offering evidence of ecological change long before human arrival.

ADDED NOVEMBER 10, 2026 • RESEARCH PUBLISHED JANUARY 2026

A rare glimpse of the Early Pleistocene

Trevor Worthy and colleagues describe a terrestrial vertebrate assemblage from Moa Eggshell Cave, near Waitomo on New Zealand’s North Island. It contains 12 species-level bird taxa and four species of native Leiopelma frogs, filling an important gap in the country’s fossil record.

How the deposits were dated

The geological constraints include ash from the 1.55-million-year-old Ngaroma eruption, the roughly one-million-year-old Kidnappers eruption, and a cave mineral deposit dated to 535,000 years ago on the sediment’s upper surface. These markers constrain the sequence; they are not three separate direct dates on every bone.

A changing bird community

The paper describes two new species: the parrot Strigops insulaborealis and the rail Porphyrio claytongreenei. At least four, and probably six, of the 12 bird taxa are absent from known Late Pleistocene assemblages. That comparison underlies the reported 33–50% bird-species turnover.

The authors associate this change with stronger glacial–interglacial fluctuations and suggest that volcanism also played a role. The result does not establish that every animal represented in the cave disappeared together.

A possible flying kākāpō relative

Canterbury Museum’s announcement highlights the ancient Strigops as a kākāpō relative that may have been able to fly. Its leg anatomy differs from the modern flightless bird’s, but the announcement explicitly says further work is needed to confirm flight capability.

Timeline: The paper and institutional announcements appeared in January 2026. November coverage revisits that research.

MKP reflection

A fossil assemblage is a sample of a past community, shaped by preservation and discovery. Comparing it with later samples can reveal change, but explaining that change requires additional evidence. How could researchers distinguish the effects of a major eruption from gradual habitat shifts—and from an incomplete fossil record?
