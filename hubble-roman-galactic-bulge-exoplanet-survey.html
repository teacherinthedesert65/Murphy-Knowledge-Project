Hubble Maps the Milky Way for Roman’s Planet Hunt

Two space telescopes combine observations to reveal hidden planetary systems.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 27, 2026

Mapping the stars before the hunt

NASA released Hubble images of the galactic bulge on November 25, 2026. The observing program led by Sean Terry provides a reference survey in red visible and near-infrared wavelengths that overlap Roman's sensitivity. The central concentration contains nearly a quarter of the Milky Way's stellar mass.

Gravity as a telescope

When a foreground object passes sufficiently close to the line of sight to a distant star, its gravity magnifies the background light. A planet orbiting the foreground star can introduce a short anomaly in the light curve. Isolated planets can also generate lensing events without a luminous host.

From mass ratios to physical masses

Hubble's earlier images help separate sources that later overlap and constrain their colors, brightness and motion. Combining lens flux, proper motion and lensing models can improve estimates of individual masses. For example, if the measured planet-to-star mass ratio is 0.001 and the host mass is 0.8 solar masses, the planet mass is approximately 0.84 Jupiter masses. This is an illustrative calculation, not a reported discovery. Dark lenses and crowded fields can still leave ambiguities.

Roman's observing strategy

The planned high-frequency survey spans six 72-day seasons, taking images roughly every 12 minutes over about 1.7 square degrees. The cadence helps capture short-lived planetary signals. The program complements transit searches by probing planets farther from their stars and potentially free-floating worlds, as well as neutron stars and black holes. These are survey goals rather than completed detections.

MKP perspective

Combining observatories across time demonstrates why astronomical archives are valuable scientific infrastructure. Publicly accessible precursor data can enable independent tests of lens models and improve estimates of detection completeness. A census of planets requires correcting for selection effects, not merely counting discoveries.

Discussion question

How would you distinguish a free-floating planet from a planet orbiting a distant, undetected host star?
