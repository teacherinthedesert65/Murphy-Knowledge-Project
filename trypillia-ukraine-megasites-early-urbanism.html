Trypillia’s Mega-Sites: Rethinking How Early Cities Could Work

Huge planned settlements in present-day Ukraine raise questions about food, cooperation, population, and what makes a city.

ADDED NOVEMBER 18, 2026

A settlement on a surprising scale

Around 6,000 years ago, Trypillia communities created very large settlements in what is now Ukraine. Kiel University describes planned mega-sites beginning around 4150 BCE, with some reaching approximately 320 hectares. Its research summary cites estimates of up to 15,000 inhabitants, neighbourhoods, and communal meeting buildings.

These are Copper Age communities, not an unknown modern city buried intact. Their importance lies in the scale of collective settlement and the questions it poses about how people organised daily life.

Why the population number needs a qualifier

An archaeological plan records buildings constructed over time. Estimating population requires assumptions about how many were occupied together, household size, and seasonal attendance.

Research at the 238-hectare Nebelivka site found relatively modest environmental impacts in pollen, charcoal and sediment evidence. Its authors considered smaller or less permanent populations, including seasonal assembly models. That result cannot simply be applied unchanged to every Trypillia mega-site, but it shows why 15,000 should remain an estimate rather than a census.

Cooperation without a familiar skyline

A 2024 Antiquity study uses house-size comparisons to investigate inequality. It proposes social levelling mechanisms and cooperative organisation, while interpreting later differentiation as part of the explanation for abandonment.

What this does not establish: Similar-sized houses are evidence relevant to inequality, not a complete political constitution. Calling these places cities also depends on how urbanism is defined. Their scale challenges a single-path account of urban development; it does not settle a contest for the world’s “first city.”

Food makes the achievement concrete

Kiel’s food-supply research emphasises legumes, manure-supported cultivation and a predominantly plant-based diet. Large communities needed reliable provisioning as well as buildings. Think of the repeated tasks: planting, harvesting, storing, managing animals and coordinating access. A settlement’s footprint is visible evidence of an organisation whose everyday operation must be reconstructed.

MKP reflection: size is the beginning of the question

A large place does not automatically require one familiar form of rule. The interesting comparison is between possible ways to coordinate people: neighbourhood decisions, shared gathering spaces, household production, or central institutions. Archaeology tests these possibilities through material traces.

Discussion question: What would convince you that thousands of people lived together permanently rather than gathering periodically? Consider food waste, water use, building dates and changes in surrounding vegetation.
