How Much Farther Away Is the Moon Than in 1969?

About 2.2 metres of long-term retreat since Apollo 11—measured through decades of lunar laser ranging.

ADDED NOVEMBER 7, 2026 • CALCULATION THROUGH NOVEMBER 7, 2026

The short answer: a little over seven feet

NASA reports that the Moon’s mean distance from Earth increases by about 3.8 centimetres per year. Applying that rate from Apollo 11’s July 20, 1969 landing to November 7, 2026 gives approximately 2.18 metres, or 7.1 feet.

This is a calculated estimate of the long-term trend. It is not the difference between two individual Earth–Moon distance measurements taken on those dates.

Check the calculation

About 57.3 years × 3.8 centimetres per year = 217.7 centimetres, which rounds to 2.2 metres. The extra decimal places in the arithmetic do not make the underlying rate exact.

How scientists measure it

Apollo astronauts left retroreflectors on the lunar surface. Ground observatories send laser pulses toward these arrays and time the faint returning light. Knowing light’s speed allows researchers to calculate the distance: one-way range is approximately light speed multiplied by round-trip time, divided by two.

The light takes roughly 2.5 seconds to make the round trip. Repeated measurements over decades reveal orbital changes, rather than relying on a ruler or on photographs of the Moon’s apparent size.

Why the Moon retreats

Tidal interaction transfers angular momentum from Earth’s rotation to the Moon’s orbit. Earth’s rotating tidal bulge pulls forward on the Moon, increasing its orbit; the corresponding torque slows Earth’s rotation. Over long periods, this contributes to longer days.

A slow trend inside a changing orbit

The Moon follows an elliptical orbit. NASA gives average closest and farthest distances of roughly 363,400 and 405,500 kilometres. That variation of tens of thousands of kilometres dwarfs the few metres of long-term retreat since 1969.

Consequently, the Moon can be closer to Earth on one particular day today than it was on another day decades ago, while its orbit still expands over the long term.

What the number does—and does not—tell us

A useful measurement needs a clearly defined comparison. Here, the question concerns a gradual orbital trend, rather than the Moon’s instantaneous location. The modern recession rate also should not be extended unchanged across billions of years: tidal behavior depends on the evolving Earth–Moon system.

For discussion: how can a repeating rise and fall hide a small steady change? What length of record would help separate the two?
