Hera Reveals the Aftermath of NASA’s Asteroid Deflection Test

Four years after DART, close-up observations reveal how a deliberate collision transformed a small asteroid.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 25, 2026

What Hera discovered

New observations reported on November 25 show a large impact basin on the approximately 151-meter-wide moonlet Dimorphos. Much of the struck hemisphere has changed shape. Hundreds of nearby rocks and an apparently unstable rotation complicate the picture. The origin and trajectories of individual fragments and the nature of features inside the basin require further investigation.

Why DART mattered

NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test deliberately collided with Dimorphos in September 2022, shortening its orbital period around Didymos by about 33 minutes. Neither asteroid posed a threat to Earth. Material expelled by an impact can contribute additional recoil, making a deflection more effective than the spacecraft's momentum alone.

What Hera will investigate next

Hera will study the mass, surface and interior of the moonlet, alongside the larger, roughly 730-meter-wide Didymos. Two small spacecraft, Milani and Juventas, will support composition measurements and radar investigations. Detailed measurements will help determine how internal structure controls an asteroid's response to a collision.

MKP perspective

Planetary defense requires not just the ability to move an asteroid but the ability to predict how it will respond. The DART experiment established the feasibility of kinetic deflection in one system. Hera helps quantify the uncertainties that matter when designing a mission against a real threat. A successful experiment does not mean that every asteroid can be redirected in the same way.

Discussion question

How might a loosely bound asteroid respond differently from a solid rock, and how could early detection improve our ability to protect Earth?
