Hearing the Laschamps Event: Earth’s Ancient Magnetic Field Turned Into Sound

An audiovisual reconstruction explores a magnetic excursion about 41,000 years ago, using scientific models and an artistic soundscape.

ADDED NOVEMBER 17, 2026

A brief upheaval in Earth’s magnetic field

About 41,000 years ago, Earth experienced the Laschamps event, also called the Laschamp event. Its magnetic field weakened dramatically and briefly reversed. ESA describes the minimum strength as roughly 5% of today’s value, allowing more cosmic rays to reach the atmosphere.

This was a geomagnetic excursion: the field ultimately returned to its original polarity. A lasting polarity reversal has a different outcome. Neither event means the solid Earth physically flips over.

Listen to the reconstruction

Watch and hear ESA’s 80-second Laschamps visualization →

Released on October 10, 2024, the piece combines work from Germany’s GFZ and DTU Space in Denmark. ESA credits Maximilian Arthus Schanner and Guram Kervalishvili for the animation and science, and Klaus Nielsen for the sound. Recordings such as creaking wood and tumbling rocks become a changing soundtrack.

Data translated into sound

The team’s methods paper explains that the reconstruction uses a global model informed by ancient magnetic records in lava and sediments. Modern satellite observations help us understand today’s field; satellites did not directly observe this prehistoric event.

The animation compresses the period from 42,500 to 39,500 years ago into 80 seconds. In the sonification, data control playback properties of processed recordings. For example, stronger modeled field intensity increases sound volume.

A representation, not a recording: Nobody captured a microphone track 41,000 years ago. The soundtrack combines data mapping with artistic decisions. Its unsettling mood is not a measurement of how frightening the event was, and it does not forecast an imminent reversal.

MKP reflection

Sonification offers another way to explore information that is difficult to sense directly. It can make change easier to notice and invite listeners into scientific questions. The strongest presentations also explain their mapping choices: what gets louder, what changes pitch, and what the sound cannot tell us.

Discussion question

If you chose different instruments or mapped field strength to pitch instead of volume, how might the same data change a listener’s interpretation?
