Hayabusa2 Changes Course: A Flyby Instead of a Rendezvous

Engine trouble reshapes Japan’s extended asteroid mission—and reveals the difference between passing a world and keeping pace with it.

ADDED NOVEMBER 17, 2026

A different encounter, with a different propulsion budget

JAXA’s November 17, 2026 briefing replaces Hayabusa2’s planned 2031 rendezvous with asteroid 1998 KY26 with an April 2028 flyby of 2015 BM510. The new target is classified as a dark D-type asteroid and a fast rotator. This is a future mission plan, not a completed encounter.

What happened to the engines?

JAXA’s September engineering report traces the problem to repeated electrical discharges in engine B during August, followed by confirmation of a power-supply fault. Engineers demonstrated an alternative: using engine D’s ion source with engine B’s neutralizer. These components normally work together within one engine; the cross-operation combines usable parts from separate units.

That makes “all propulsion is gone” an incomplete description. The harder question is how much dependable maneuvering remains. In November, JAXA identified uncertain remaining life in cross-operation and selected a trajectory compatible with the chemical thrusters.

Put the maneuvering requirement side by side

The November briefing gives these planning figures. Delta-v means a change in velocity, including direction—not the spacecraft’s total cruising speed.

JAXA’s November 2026 trajectory comparison
Planning itemApproximate delta-v
Maneuvering toward the old plan’s 2027 Earth swing-by200 m/s
Dependable chemical-thruster capability30 m/s
2015 BM510 flyby candidate24 m/s, subject to refinement

For scale: 200 is about 6.7 times 30. The 24 m/s candidate fits within that stated chemical capability; this arithmetic illustrates the selection, rather than proving every operational margin. The 200 m/s figure concerns the swing-by preparation, not the entire old mission.

Passing by versus keeping pace

Why the encounter type matters
QuestionFlybyRendezvous
What must the spacecraft do?Cross the target’s path at the right moment.Reach the target and closely match its motion.
Everyday comparisonPhotographing a landmark while passing it.Slowing down to travel alongside another vehicle.
Observational tradeoffA brief close encounter demands careful timing.Staying nearby allows repeated observations over time.

The analogy explains relative motion; it does not reproduce orbital mechanics. A spacecraft can coast through space while still lacking the thrust budget to reach a particular destination on schedule. Fuel remaining and a reachable trajectory are different requirements.

MKP reflection: adapting the question to the tools

A changed destination means losing some opportunities and preserving others. A short encounter can reveal a surface and shape that distant observations cannot resolve, while leaving questions that require sustained monitoring unanswered. Readers should judge the revised mission by the observations it can realistically deliver.

Hayabusa2 already returned Ryugu samples to Earth in 2020. Its extended mission builds on that achievement; the engine problem does not undo the earlier sample return.

Discussion question: If your instruments still work but your maneuvering capability shrinks, would you prioritize a difficult brief encounter or a longer, less ambitious observation? What evidence would guide your choice?
