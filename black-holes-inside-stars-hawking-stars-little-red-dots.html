Black Holes Inside Stars? Two Ideas, Different Evidence

How can light reveal a hidden engine—and when is a detection strategy still only a proposal?

ADDED NOVEMBER 20, 2026 • RESEARCH FROM 2023 AND 2026

A dramatic phrase combines two different stories

“Black holes consuming stars from the inside out” can blur distinct scientific ideas. One concerns a hypothetical tiny primordial black hole inside an otherwise ordinary star. Another concerns distant objects called little red dots, some interpreted as growing massive black holes surrounded by thick gas. Neither story should be presented as direct observation of a black hole eating a familiar star’s interior.

Hawking stars: reading an interior through vibrations

Max Planck researchers reported stellar-evolution models in December 2023 exploring what happens if a forming star captures a small primordial black hole. Such black holes are hypothesized relics of the early universe; their existence has not been established. The modeled star can remain surprisingly similar to a normal star for long periods. Growth depends on how matter and energy move around the central object, rather than an automatic, instantaneous swallowing of the whole star.

The proposed test is asteroseismology: using patterns of stellar oscillations to infer internal structure. A black hole’s influence on the core and energy transport could leave a distinctive pattern, especially later in the star’s life. This is a search strategy arising from models, not a report that a black hole has been found inside the Sun. These oscillations are measured through changes in light or surface motion—not sound traveling through empty space.

Little red dots: dissecting light from dense gas

Webb’s little red dots are real observations, although their engines remain debated. NASA’s June 2026 account of GLIMPSE-17775 describes more than 40 spectral lines. Electron scattering helps explain broad line profiles; iron, oxygen, and helium features support a powerful source embedded in dense gas. In the black-hole-star model, gas absorbs and reprocesses radiation from accretion. This is not the same scenario as a primordial black hole captured inside a Sun-like star.

Missing X-rays are a constraint, not a verdict

Many little red dots are unexpectedly faint in X-rays compared with ordinary active black holes. Dense gas could hide that emission. But April 2026 Chandra results identified 3DHST-AEGIS-12014, a red-dot-like source that does emit X-rays. Researchers propose gaps in an obscuring envelope as one explanation; an unusual dust-obscured active black hole is another. This possible transition object has not settled the population’s identity.

A competing model highlighted by Harvard & Smithsonian in August 2026 invokes supermassive stars whose pulsations eject compact gas shells. It seeks to explain spectra, compactness, and nitrogen enrichment before the star collapses into a black-hole seed. Thus, X-ray faintness alone cannot establish an already-existing black hole. Different objects may also require different explanations.

MKP reflection: require a whole pattern of evidence

A missing signal becomes persuasive when a model predicts how much should have been detectable and also explains the signals that are present. Spectra, variability, and observations at different wavelengths must work together. The useful question is not simply “Could a black hole do this?” but “Which measurement could distinguish that explanation from its competitors?”

Discussion: What would be a stronger test: one unusual feature or several independently predicted features in the same object?
