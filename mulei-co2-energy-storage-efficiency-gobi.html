ENERGY STORAGE • THERMODYNAMICS • CHECKING CLAIMS

A CO₂ Battery in the Gobi: What the Energy Numbers Tell Us

Storage returns less electricity than it receives. Its value depends on when that electricity is available, what it costs, and what it displaces.

ADDED NOVEMBER 21, 2026 • GRID-CONNECTION ANNOUNCEMENTS OCTOBER 2026

A real project, with projected annual figures

China Huadian reports that its compressed-carbon-dioxide storage project in Mulei, Xinjiang, first connected to the grid on September 30, 2026. Its October 1 announcement gives annual figures of 290 million kWh stored during low-demand periods and 180 million kWh released at peak demand. These are forward-looking statements about the plant’s capability, not a year of audited operating data.

How CO₂ stores electricity

The operator describes a closed loop: electricity drives compression, compression heat is stored, and the CO₂ is condensed for storage. During discharge, the fluid is vaporized and reheated using stored heat, then expanded through a turbine to generate electricity. Here “battery” describes an energy-storage function, rather than the electrochemical process of a lithium-ion cell. The CO₂ is a working fluid; this is not a claim that the plant removes atmospheric carbon.

Engineering contractor Dongfang Electric describes a cycle without supplementary fuel combustion and above-ground gas storage that avoids dependence on suitable underground formations. Its announcement associates the project with 600 MW of wind and 400 MW of solar. Those generation ratings are not the storage plant’s annual energy output.

If both annual numbers refer to comparable electricity-metering boundaries and the same operating plan, they imply roughly 62% electricity return. They do not establish a certified round-trip efficiency test. The announcement does not provide enough detail to reconcile auxiliary loads, operating assumptions, or measurement boundaries.

One important correction to the headline: The original operator announcement does print both figures. Readers can calculate their ratio. A separate statement in that same release gives approximately 200 million kWh of annual generation; its relationship to the 180 million kWh figure is not explained. These numbers should not be silently treated as identical.

Turbine efficiency is not plant efficiency

Dongfang reports turbine efficiency above 90%. A turbine converts only one part of the cycle. That percentage does not include all charging, heat-storage, and plant-operation losses, so it cannot replace the whole-system energy ratio. Comparing different efficiency boundaries is a common source of misleading claims.

Can losing energy still be useful?

Yes, in principle. Electricity that would otherwise be curtailed can gain value when shifted to a period of demand. But usefulness is not demonstrated by the efficiency ratio alone. A fair assessment also needs costs, availability, cycling performance, and the generation actually displaced.

Using the 62.1% ratio as a simplified example, purchased charging electricity at 3 cents per kWh would cost about 4.83 cents per returned kWh before capital costs, maintenance, and other charges. This is an illustration, not a claim about the project’s tariffs or profitability. It shows why input energy price matters alongside efficiency.

MKP reflection

Technology deserves credit for what it achieves and scrutiny for what its measurements mean. Grid connection is a milestone; sustained performance needs operating evidence. The useful next question is what independent, consistently metered results show after extended service.

Discussion: Which matters most for a particular grid: energy returned, cost per delivered kWh, discharge duration, or reliability—and how would you compare those trade-offs?
