A Whale Genome From a Breath: Drones Open a Gentler Sampling Route

Humpback whale blow yields usable genomic data, with repeat samples and tissue comparisons testing the method’s reliability.

ADDED NOVEMBER 17, 2026

From exhaled droplets to genetic information

Research announced by the University of St Andrews on November 17, 2026, reports genomic sequencing from humpback whale “blow”: the droplets released when whales breathe at the surface. Sterile Petri dishes on a small drone captured samples in the Kitimat Fjord System, in Gitga’at First Nation territory in northern British Columbia.

The team collected 58 samples from 26 whales. That distinction matters: 58 samples are not 58 different animals. Repeated sampling helps test whether the method consistently identifies the same individual.

What does “whole genome” mean here?

The lead researcher’s thesis reports average nuclear sequencing depth of 2.3×, ranging from 0.2× to 3.5×, and mitochondrial depth averaging 94×. About 84% of the DNA reads came from the whale itself.

Coverage is like reading a huge book through overlapping fragments. An average depth of 2.3× does not mean every letter was read exactly 2.3 times—or that every position was read at all. Whole-genome sequencing samples across the genome; it is not automatically a gap-free, chromosome-by-chromosome reconstruction. Low-depth data can still support population analyses when uncertainty is handled appropriately.

Nuclear DNA contains information inherited from both parents. The much smaller mitochondrial genome follows the maternal line. Their different coverage values describe different genetic datasets, not a 94-fold improvement in the whale’s entire genome.

Breath sampling versus a tissue biopsy

Two routes to genetic evidence
QuestionDrone-collected blowBiopsy sampling
Material collectedExhaled droplets containing genetic materialA small skin-and-blubber sample
Physical interventionNo tissue removal; drone approaches the blowA sampling dart removes tissue, usually from a nearby boat
What this study testedWhether blow provides repeatable genomic evidencePaired tissue samples provided a comparison for some whales

Validation makes the result significant

The university reports agreement between repeated blow samples and between blow and biopsy data from the same animals. Researchers targeted the upwind whale to reduce mixing, avoided resting whales, and stopped flights when an animal reacted. More than one-third of samples were collected with land-launched flights.

These precautions make “non-invasive” more meaningful, but the term does not guarantee zero disturbance. Nor does success with these humpbacks establish equal performance in every whale species or setting. Breath collection provides another option rather than making every tissue sample obsolete.

MKP reflection

A breath can provide clues to genetic diversity, relationships, and population structure without removing tissue. The advance is not simply that DNA was present: the researchers checked whether the resulting information held up against independent samples. That combination of gentler collection and validation is what makes the method promising for conservation.

Genomic information is one part of monitoring a population. It does not by itself measure food supply, ship-strike risk, or an individual whale’s complete health.

Discussion question

Would you prioritize deeper sequencing of fewer whales, or shallower sequencing of more individuals? How would your choice change if you wanted to identify relatives rather than investigate one rare genetic variant?
