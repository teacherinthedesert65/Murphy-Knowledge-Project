Mars ISRU: Learning to Live Off the Land

Human settlement on Mars becomes far more practical if crews can make oxygen, water, propellant, and construction materials from resources already present on the planet.

Murphy Knowledge Project • Reviewed September 2026

In-situ resource utilization, or ISRU, is the idea of using local materials at a destination instead of transporting every kilogram from Earth. For Mars, that means treating the atmosphere, regolith, subsurface ice, sunlight, and locally available minerals as parts of an industrial supply chain.

Why ISRU matters

Every kilogram launched from Earth carries a transportation penalty. For a short mission, heavy dependence on resupply may be acceptable. For a long-lived settlement, it becomes a weakness. ISRU changes the mission architecture by shifting some critical supplies from cargo to production.

AtmosphereMars is rich in carbon dioxide, which can be processed into oxygen and potentially used as part of fuel-production systems.
WaterWater ice and hydrated materials are potential sources for drinking water, crop production, oxygen, and propellant chemistry.
RegolithMartian soil and rock may support shielding, construction, ceramics, metals, and other manufacturing feedstocks.

MOXIE proved the basic oxygen pathway

NASA's MOXIE experiment aboard Perseverance demonstrated that oxygen can be extracted from the carbon-dioxide-rich Martian atmosphere. Across 16 runs, MOXIE produced 122 grams of oxygen and reached up to 12 grams per hour at high purity. The experiment was a technology demonstration, not a settlement-scale plant, but it showed that the core chemistry works on Mars.

What a settlement-scale system would need

A practical ISRU plant would need far more than a single processor. It would need excavation or atmospheric intake, purification, power, thermal control, storage, maintenance, redundancy, and quality monitoring. Water extraction would have to cope with uncertain deposits and changing geology. Propellant production would need reliable chemical processing and cryogenic or pressure storage.

The deeper challenge: reliability

ISRU only reduces mission risk if the hardware is dependable. A crew cannot rely on a machine that works only under ideal laboratory conditions. For Project ALCHEMY, the design principle is therefore not simply “make it locally,” but “make it locally with backup pathways, repairable components, and measurable production margins.”
