35 Rembrandt Etchings Rediscovered Among Family Keepsakes

Charlotte Meyer’s inherited prints became a museum exhibition—and a window into Rembrandt’s art, printmaking and family collecting.

ADDED NOVEMBER 8, 2026 • REDISCOVERY IN 2020

A family collection seen anew

During the pandemic in 2020, Dutch collector Charlotte Meyer revisited prints inherited from her grandfather. Reporting in Smithsonian describes a folder containing 35 Rembrandt etchings, acquired between 1900 and 1920 for modest sums. What had seemed like attractive family possessions became the foundation of a much larger collecting project.

The Stedelijk Museum Zutphen confirms that Meyer inherited her grandfather’s collection through her mother and recognized the Rembrandt works in 2020. Its own account describes their storage in a vault; news accounts emphasize a folder in a drawer. The central discovery is the recognition of an existing family collection’s significance.

From private storage to public view

The museum presented Rembrandt, From Darkness to Light from March 21 through June 14, 2026. By then Meyer’s collection had grown to more than 70 Rembrandt etchings. Works by his predecessors, contemporaries and followers placed his art in a wider historical setting.

The timeline matters: This is a rediscovery from 2020 and an exhibition that has already ended. November coverage brings the story to new readers.

How can a print be an original?

An etching begins with a design on a prepared metal plate. Acid bites exposed lines into the metal, and ink held in those recesses transfers to paper under pressure. The Rijksmuseum explains that a copper plate can produce multiple impressions and that Rembrandt sometimes altered his plates between printings.

That makes printmaking different from creating a single painting. Multiple impressions can belong to the artist’s original printmaking practice. However, an image bearing Rembrandt’s design does not by itself establish when a particular sheet was printed. Attribution, printing history and condition still require specialist examination.

MKP reflection

The most interesting part of this story may be what happened after recognition: a private inheritance became a reason to research, collect and share. What family objects would you document today so that future generations could understand their origins and significance?
