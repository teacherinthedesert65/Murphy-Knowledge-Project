Robotics and the New Age of Automation

Robots are moving beyond tightly scripted factory motions toward systems that combine sensing, software, machine vision, and increasingly flexible decision-making.

Murphy Knowledge Project • Reviewed September 2026

A robot is more than a moving machine. Useful robotic systems connect perception, control, mechanical design, software, and safety so a machine can act reliably in the physical world.

From fixed automation to flexible systems

Traditional industrial robots excel at repetitive tasks in structured environments. Modern systems increasingly use cameras, force sensors, better control software, and AI-assisted perception so they can adapt to variation, work alongside people, and be retasked more quickly.

SensingCameras, force sensors, encoders, lidar, and other instruments tell a robot about itself and its surroundings.
ControlSoftware converts goals into precise motion while maintaining stability, accuracy, and safe operating limits.
PlanningMore advanced systems choose routes, grasps, task sequences, or responses when the environment changes.

Why factories remain the proving ground

Manufacturing rewards repeatability, throughput, and precision, which makes it a natural environment for robotics. NIST notes that automation can improve consistency, productivity, production capacity, and worker safety when it is matched to the right process.

Collaborative robots

Collaborative robots, often called cobots, are designed for closer interaction with human workers than traditional caged industrial systems. That does not mean they are automatically safe in every situation. Safe deployment still depends on task design, speed, force, tooling, workspace layout, risk assessment, and appropriate safeguards.

The hard part is the real world

A demo can succeed under ideal conditions while a production system struggles with glare, clutter, worn parts, unexpected obstacles, variable objects, network failures, or edge cases. Robust robotics is therefore as much about handling uncertainty and failure as it is about performing the nominal task.

The most important shift in robotics is not simply “more intelligence.” It is the integration of better sensing, flexible software, safer human interaction, and systems that can recover when the physical world does not behave exactly as expected.

Where this is heading

Warehouse robots, autonomous inspection, agricultural machines, assistive systems, surgical robotics, and general-purpose mobile manipulators all build on the same underlying challenge: turning digital decisions into dependable physical action.
