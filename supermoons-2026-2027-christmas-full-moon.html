A Season of Supermoons—and 2026’s Closest Full Moon Before Christmas

A skywatching guide to the November–February full moons, the December highlight, and why supermoon counts vary.

ADDED NOVEMBER 13, 2026

December’s highlight

The closest full Moon of 2026 arrives just before Christmas. Astronomer Fred Espenak’s ephemeris places full phase at December 24, 01:28 UTC—December 23 at 5:28 p.m. Pacific Standard Time. Its geocentric distance at full phase is approximately 356,740 kilometers. The Moon reaches perigee several hours later, so “full Moon” and “closest approach” are separate moments.

Times mark the instant of full phase, not local moonrise. The disk looks nearly full on neighboring evenings too. Published calculations may differ by about a minute.

Three supermoons or four?

“Supermoon” is an informal label for a full Moon near perigee, the near point of its elliptical orbit. There is no single official astronomical cutoff. NASA’s November skywatching guide describes three consecutive supermoons: November, December, and January. Espenak’s broader perigee-full-moon table also includes February. Different thresholds explain the “up to four” wording.

What will it look like?

A nearby full Moon appears somewhat larger and brighter, but the change can be subtle without a comparison image. The dramatic impression of a huge Moon near the horizon is the Moon illusion, which also occurs with ordinary full moons.

NASA recommends looking east around sunset for November’s rising Moon. For any observing evening, check local weather and moonrise timing; the exact full-phase instant does not guarantee that the Moon is above your horizon.

MKP reflection

This is a useful example of a headline combining three different ideas: a precisely calculated phase, a changing orbital distance, and an informal category. Keeping them separate makes the event easier to understand—and easier to plan for.
