Can Red Turbine Blades Protect Birds? The Ecowende Offshore Experiment

A Dutch offshore wind farm tests whether contrasting rotor blades can reduce bird collisions.

MKP RESEARCH EXPLAINER • NOVEMBER 27, 2026

Renewable energy meets wildlife conservation

Ecowende, a joint venture of Shell, Eneco and Chubu, installed its final wind turbine on October 30, 2026, approximately 53 kilometres offshore from IJmuiden in the Netherlands. The project generated its first electricity in July and expects full operation by year-end. Its stated operational capacity is approximately 760 MW. The 52 turbines are individually rated at 15 MW, giving a summed nameplate rating of 780 MW; this is distinct from the reported project capacity.

Why paint one blade red?

Seven turbines each have one red blade. The contrasting appearance is intended to help birds perceive the rotating rotor rather than encounter a visually indistinct blur. Red was selected after consideration of material durability and heat resistance. The effect on collision rates remains unproven at this site.

Testing the hypothesis

Radar, thermal and daylight cameras and impact sensors will support ecological monitoring. A useful comparison would measure collisions and bird passages at painted and unpainted turbines, accounting for species, weather, migration, operating hours and sensor detection rates. Rare collisions may require extensive monitoring to establish a reliable effect.

Beyond blade colour

The project incorporates wider turbine spacing along a bird flight corridor, greater rotor clearance at selected locations, and adaptive shutdowns supported by AI-assisted radar identification. Underwater ecological trials include reef structures, fish refuges and native oyster restoration.

MKP perspective

Renewable infrastructure should be judged by measured environmental outcomes as well as energy production. Publishing collision rates, uncertainty intervals and electricity losses from protective shutdowns would help engineers identify measures that offer the greatest ecological benefit per unit of energy delivered.

Discussion question

How would you design a controlled field experiment to determine whether blade colour, rather than turbine location or bird abundance, explains a difference in collision rates?
