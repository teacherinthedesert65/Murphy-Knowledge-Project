Fusion’s Confinement Switch: A New Theory for the Heating Threshold

Why does adding heat sometimes make a plasma better at keeping its heat? A new framework connects several clues to this longstanding puzzle.

ADDED NOVEMBER 20, 2026 • PAPER NOVEMBER 13 • EPFL REPORT NOVEMBER 20

The question behind the headline

In 1982, researchers discovered that enough heating can shift a tokamak plasma from low confinement, or L-mode, into high confinement, or H-mode. Turbulence near the edge decreases, improving energy retention. Predicting the required power has remained difficult because the threshold depends on density and magnetic configuration.

A Swiss Plasma Center team at EPFL, led by Brenno De Lucca, now offers a unified physical explanation. The work was published in Physical Review Letters on November 13, 2026. “Solving a 40-year puzzle” describes progress on this specific transition, not the completion of fusion power technology.

How turbulence can help suppress itself

The researchers used simulations, mathematical analysis, and comparisons with experimental measurements. Their account describes turbulence generating flows whose speeds vary across the plasma’s outer region. This shear suppresses turbulent transport, reducing heat leakage. A useful analogy is neighboring lanes moving at different speeds and disrupting a swirling pattern that tries to span them; the plasma mechanism itself involves electric and magnetic fields.

The paper identifies electromagnetic drift-wave turbulence and a sheared E × B flow. It also links the difference between favorable and unfavorable toroidal-field directions to collisions and the system’s geometry and flows. Its scaling laws cover both density branches and the density at which the heating requirement is lowest. Thus, increasing density does not always move the threshold in the same direction.

Three distinct claims
ClaimWhat it means
H-mode is observedA real improvement in confinement, known for decades.
A model explains the thresholdA testable framework for how operating conditions affect access to H-mode.
A power plant delivers electricityA much broader engineering and energy-balance achievement, not demonstrated by this study.

What the estimates imply for future machines

The authors’ accessible preprint estimates minimum threshold powers of 43 ± 11 MW for ITER and 26 ± 7 MW for SPARC in specified full-field scenarios. It compares these with planned auxiliary-heating capacities of 73 MW and 25 MW, respectively. The quoted uncertainties are estimated from model error; these are extrapolations, not measurements on those future operating scenarios.

Simple subtraction puts the central estimates 30 MW below ITER’s cited capacity and 1 MW above SPARC’s. Those comparisons suggest different planning margins. The uncertainty on SPARC’s prediction is larger than the 1 MW difference, so it would be wrong to turn that central estimate into a verdict that the machine cannot enter H-mode.

Read “predict” carefully: A heating-threshold estimate is conditional on the model and operating scenario. H-mode means improved confinement; it does not promise the absence of every instability. Auxiliary heating power is also not electrical output or proof of net electricity generation.

MKP reflection

A stronger model does more than fit a familiar curve: it explains why changing an input changes the outcome, then risks a prediction in a new setting. The next test is how well these estimates hold under additional experimental conditions. Progress can sharpen design choices before it produces a working power station.

Discussion: When a planned heating capacity lies close to a predicted threshold, what additional measurements would most help engineers judge the margin?
