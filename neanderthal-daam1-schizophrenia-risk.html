A Neanderthal DNA Variant, Brain Anatomy, and Schizophrenia Risk

A PNAS study links a specific inherited variant near DAAM1 to small differences in brain anatomy and lower schizophrenia risk.

ADDED NOVEMBER 16, 2026

What the new study found

Hugo Zeberg and Svante Pääbo’s study, published in PNAS on November 16, 2026, identifies a common variant inherited from Neanderthals near DAAM1, a gene involved in brain development. The authors connect it with altered gene expression and an average occipital-lobe volume difference of about 0.85 cubic centimeters less per copy.

People with two copies showed an association with almost 10% lower schizophrenia risk. Neanderthal ancestry overall did not show the same pattern. This concerns a particular inherited variant, rather than a general protective effect of having more Neanderthal DNA.

The authors found little or no association with general cognitive ability. Any educational association was very small: at most roughly two weeks per copy against an average of about 15 years of schooling.

Reading “10% lower” carefully

A relative reduction is different from a percentage-point reduction. As a purely illustrative calculation, reducing a 1% risk by 10% gives 0.9%, not a drop of ten percentage points. That example is not a personalized estimate from this study.

An association also does not establish the entire biological pathway. The results do not show that a smaller occipital lobe itself prevents schizophrenia, or that changing DAAM1 would be an effective treatment. Nor does a population average determine an individual person’s anatomy, abilities, or future diagnosis.

MKP reflection

The finding connects ancient human history with questions about present-day biology. Its value is a testable lead for research. A brain-region measurement alone is not a measure of intelligence or human worth, and an inherited variant is not a diagnosis.

Evidence scope: This explainer uses the authors’ publisher-deposited abstract, available through Crossref. The full article and supplementary analyses were not accessible during preparation; detailed causal mechanisms remain outside what was verified here.

Discussion question
