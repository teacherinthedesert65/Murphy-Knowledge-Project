Engineering Living Partnerships: Plants, Fungi, Bacteria & the Future of Agriculture

Can resilient ecosystems be designed around cooperation between organisms, rather than improving one crop species at a time? What the evidence shows—and what it would take to put the idea to work.

Murphy Knowledge Project • October 13, 2026 • Evidence-based exploration

Traditional crop improvement often concentrates on the plant: breeding for yield, disease resistance or drought tolerance. But plants live inside networks of fungi, bacteria and other organisms. One exciting research direction asks whether cultivating beneficial relationships could help crops—and damaged landscapes—survive difficult conditions.

Established biologyMycorrhizal fungi and certain bacteria can influence nutrient acquisition and plant growth; biological soil crusts stabilize many dryland soils.
Active researchSelected microbial consortia, biocrust restoration, and substrate amendments are being tested in crops and extreme-environment simulants.
Still unprovenA universally reliable microbial mix for every field, or a self-sustaining food-growing ecosystem on the Moon or Mars.

1. Nature already builds partnerships

Fungi are not plants. They belong to a separate biological kingdom. Many mycorrhizal fungi associate with plant roots: the fungus receives carbon compounds from the plant and may assist with nutrient and water acquisition. Outcomes depend on the partners and their environment—benefits are not automatic.

Lichens are associations usually involving a fungus and a photosynthetic alga or cyanobacterium. They are striking examples of cooperative survival, but they are not simply a blueprint that can be copied into a farm. Biological soil crusts (biocrusts) are broader surface communities that can contain cyanobacteria, algae, lichens, mosses and other microorganisms. In many drylands, they help stabilize soil and influence carbon, nitrogen and water cycling. USGS research overview →

2. Practical possibility: rebuilding damaged land

Where dryland vegetation is sparse, erosion and nutrient loss can make recovery difficult. Researchers are investigating inoculation with locally appropriate biocrust organisms and ways to protect them while they establish. If approaches scale safely, they could support soil restoration and reduce erosion around agricultural landscapes. But field outcomes vary with rain, soil texture, temperature and disturbance; even promising inocula can fail to establish. USGS field-relevant evidence →

Real-world impact: healthier soils may help farmers and land stewards retain nutrients, protect land from wind and water erosion, and reduce the costs of degradation. Restoration is not the same as directly increasing crop yield; both should be measured separately.

3. Practical possibility: more resilient crops

A microbial consortium combines multiple compatible organisms selected for different functions—for example, nutrient solubilization, nitrogen cycling, or helping plants tolerate stress. A 2026 review of dryland microbial inoculation cautioned that heat, drought, salinity and local soils strongly affect performance. The authors argued that microbes should be matched to crop varieties and conditions rather than marketed as universal fixes. 2026 dryland-inoculation review →

Real-world impact: if replicated field trials confirm reliable gains, farmers might reduce some fertilizer inputs or lower losses under stressful conditions. Benefits must outweigh the costs of production, storage, application and monitoring. Smallholder access and regional suitability matter as much as laboratory performance.

4. Research spotlight: compost and microbes in space-soil simulants

A 2025 greenhouse study grew lettuce in simulants intended to resemble lunar and Martian mineral material. Compost improved biomass and nutrient availability. In particular experimental comparisons, a selected bacterial consortium increased leaf biomass by a reported 35% and mineral nutrient content by an average of 26%. These are controlled, Earth-based outcomes—not proof of successful farming on the Moon or Mars. Read the 2025 study →

Crucially, not every experiment supports adding microbes. A 2026 study of dwarf tomatoes grown with lunar regolith simulant reported that microbial inoculation had no statistically significant effect on yield in that trial, even though some fruit and growth outcomes were feasible in its amended substrate. Read the 2026 study →

Important distinction: Results obtained in simulated regolith under Earth gravity and controlled conditions do not establish performance in reduced gravity, a space habitat, or actual planetary regolith. Martian soils may also contain hazardous perchlorates; growing food safely would require validated contaminant control.

5. What could this mean for space agriculture?

A future sealed habitat might use plants, beneficial microbes, recycled plant residues and closely monitored growing media as part of a bioregenerative life-support system. The ambition is not to release engineered organisms onto another world; it is to test contained systems that recycle resources safely while reliably producing food.

Possible social benefits on Earth: methods developed for closed, resource-constrained habitats might inspire controlled-environment growing systems in drought-prone communities, isolated settlements or urban settings. But those transfers must be tested: a method optimized for space may be too expensive or energy-intensive for an ordinary farm.

6. Do we need genetic engineering?

Not necessarily. Researchers can first identify local beneficial communities, select compatible strains, improve substrates or plant-breeding choices, and test habitat conditions. Genetic engineering could eventually add capabilities, but it introduces extra questions about unintended ecological effects, stability, food safety, regulation and consent. Particularly for open-environment use, careful environmental risk assessment is essential. No organism should be assumed safe simply because it improves plant growth in a laboratory.

7. How we would test the concept responsibly

A useful research program would compare conventional crop care, compost or soil amendments alone, selected microbes alone, and combined treatments—with suitable controls, replication, and multi-season field trials. It would track crop yield and nutritional quality, irrigation use, fertilizer inputs, soil stability, microbial persistence, costs and any unwanted spread or changes to local ecosystems. It should publish null results as well as successes.

For space analogs, the tests should also address containment, toxicity, long-term recycling, crop safety, power consumption, system failure and planetary-protection considerations. Greenhouse results are the beginning of the evidence chain, not its end.

8. What might change for society?

The potential is broader than better crop yields. Reliable microbial ecosystem tools could influence food security, conservation, regional self-reliance and the affordability of farming. They could also create new dependencies if patented or proprietary inoculants displace locally sustainable practices. Who can afford the technology, who controls its use, and how benefits are verified should be debated alongside the science.
