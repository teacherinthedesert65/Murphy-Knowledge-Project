Mapping the Basal Ganglia: A New Cellular Atlas of the Brain

A cross-species brain atlas links cell identity, gene regulation, and location to help investigate movement, behavior, and neurological conditions.

ADDED NOVEMBER 12, 2026

A reference map at cellular resolution

On November 12, 2026, the BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network (BICAN) unveiled a major collection of studies mapping the basal ganglia. These interconnected structures deep in the brain help coordinate movement, learning, habits, and reward. The effort profiles millions of cells across humans and other mammals, combining molecular measurements with anatomical maps.

Participating institutions report approximately 25.5 million profiled cells across the broader effort. This is a collection of sampled-cell datasets, not a census of every cell in a living brain. Individual studies and atlas components use different subsets.

What makes it more than a picture?

The resource combines which genes cells express, features that regulate gene activity, and where cell types sit in brain tissue. Comparisons among human, macaque, marmoset, and mouse data can reveal shared features and species differences. Common cell names and anatomical frameworks help researchers compare results across laboratories.

Why Parkinson’s, schizophrenia, and autism researchers care

  • Parkinson’s: A cell-level reference helps researchers investigate why particular cells and circuits are vulnerable and identify more precise experimental targets.
  • Schizophrenia: Mapping genetic risk onto cell types can help narrow where researchers should investigate biological mechanisms. A genetic association alone does not establish causation.
  • Autism: Related work compares molecular programs in the putamen, part of the striatum, from autistic and neurotypical donors. It identifies cell-specific differences involving D1 striosome medium spiny neurons and astrocytes, including programs related to synapses and energy metabolism. The earlier public preprint is linked below.

An example: Hidden organization and aging

Broad Institute researchers identified six molecular zones within the striatum. Their gene-expression boundaries became less distinct with age. This illustrates how cellular mapping can uncover organization that conventional anatomical labels miss.

What the atlas does not establish

It does not provide a clinical test for these conditions or demonstrate a new treatment’s safety and effectiveness. Molecular differences may reflect causes, consequences, or adaptations; functional experiments are needed to distinguish them. Findings from donated tissue and animal models also require careful interpretation.

MKP reflection

A shared map makes a scientific question more precise: researchers can move from asking which brain region is involved to asking which cell type, regulatory program, and developmental stage matter. That precision is valuable even before it produces a treatment.

Discussion question: What experiment would turn a cell-type association into convincing evidence of a biological mechanism?
