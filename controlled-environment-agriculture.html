Controlled Environment Agriculture: Farming with More Control

Greenhouses, hydroponics, and vertical farms can give growers precise control over temperature, water, nutrients, and light—but that control comes with engineering and energy costs.

Murphy Knowledge Project • Reviewed September 2026

Controlled environment agriculture, or CEA, moves some or all crop production into structures where growers can manage conditions such as temperature, humidity, light, water, nutrients, and pest exposure.

CEA is a spectrum, not one technology

The category includes greenhouses, high tunnels, indoor farms, hydroponic systems, aeroponics, aquaponics, and vertically stacked growing systems. Some use sunlight with supplemental controls; fully indoor farms may rely heavily on electric lighting and climate-control equipment.

WaterRecirculating systems can reduce water losses and allow tighter control over nutrient delivery.
ClimateTemperature, humidity, airflow, carbon dioxide, and lighting can be adjusted to support crop growth.
ProductionProtected environments can support year-round production and reduce exposure to some weather extremes and pests.

Why interest has grown

USDA’s Economic Research Service reported substantial growth in U.S. controlled-environment operations between 2009 and 2019. Tomatoes, lettuce, and cucumbers made up a large share of production, and hydroponic systems were widely used.

The central tradeoff: control versus energy

Greater control can improve consistency and resource efficiency, but fully indoor systems may consume significant electricity for lighting, cooling, heating, dehumidification, and air movement. USDA Agricultural Research Service researchers note that giving up free sunlight is one of vertical farming’s major disadvantages, especially when artificial lighting and HVAC systems are needed.

Water efficiency does not solve every problem

Hydroponic and recirculating systems can use water efficiently, but they still require careful management of nutrient chemistry, sanitation, equipment reliability, root-zone conditions, and disease. Controlled environments reduce some risks while introducing others, including dependence on pumps, sensors, electricity, and climate-control hardware.

CEA works best when it is treated as an engineered agricultural system rather than as a single “future farming” technology. Crop choice, climate, electricity prices, building design, labor, water quality, and market value all affect whether a system makes sense.

Why it matters beyond Earth

The same principles become even more important in isolated habitats. Space agriculture would require tight recycling of water and nutrients, controlled lighting, environmental monitoring, and crop systems designed around scarce resources—making terrestrial CEA a useful test bed for future closed habitats.
