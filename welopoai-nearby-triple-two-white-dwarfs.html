Welopōʻai: Three Nearby Stars With a Puzzling Past

A planet search uncovered a hidden white dwarf, turning a nearby stellar pair into a rare triple system and a test of stellar evolution.

ADDED NOVEMBER 19, 2026

A discovery hiding in a planet search

Artigau and colleagues report that Welopōʻai, approximately 8.46 parsecs or 27.6 light-years away, contains a red dwarf and two white dwarfs. It is the closest known triple containing multiple white dwarfs—not the closest triple-star system of any kind.

SPIRou observations across 4.8 years revealed a roughly 16 km/s peak-to-peak radial-velocity variation. Follow-up Gemini North imaging identified the hidden inner companion. The other, more distant white dwarf was already known.

The paper gives the inner orbit a semimajor axis of 4.644 astronomical units (AU), and the outer component a projected separation of 123.9 AU. Projection on the sky is not the full three-dimensional orbit.

Put the scales in perspective

An AU is the Earth–Sun distance. On an illustrative scale where one AU becomes one metre, the inner semimajor axis becomes about 4.6 metres and the outer projected separation about 124 metres. These numbers compare two different geometric quantities, not fixed distances between stars.

Meanwhile, light takes about 27.6 years to reach us from the system. “Nearby” describes its place in the stellar neighbourhood; it does not mean close by everyday travel standards. A light-year measures distance, not age.

Why the past is difficult to explain

The IREx team estimates that the inner white dwarf’s progenitor was about 40% more massive than the Sun and expanded dramatically as a giant. The red dwarf should then have experienced a strong interaction with its companion’s envelope. Explaining its survival in the present arrangement is an open evolutionary problem.

The team also discusses possible Kozai–Lidov oscillations, in which a tilted outer orbit can alter the inner orbit’s shape and orientation. The full orbital geometry remains insufficiently constrained to establish how strong those effects are.

“Shouldn’t exist” is a challenge to an explanation: The observed system is not a violation of gravity. Its history challenges current reconstructions of how these particular stars interacted. An observation can be secure while its origin remains uncertain.

Why precise measurements matter

The paper reports an inner-white-dwarf mass near 0.618 solar masses, determined dynamically with about 0.7% uncertainty. Such measurements let astronomers test stellar models against orbital behaviour rather than relying on appearance alone.

MKP reflection: surprises can improve the question

The search began with one question about planets and led to another about stellar survival. Progress sometimes comes from noticing that an unexpected signal deserves a different explanation, then choosing an independent observation to test it.

Discussion question: Which follow-up would most help reconstruct this system’s past—better orbital geometry, more precise stellar ages, or evidence of surrounding material? What uncertainty would each address?
