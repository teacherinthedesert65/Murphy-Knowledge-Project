Two Andromeda Star Stories: Bao’s Merger and a Disputed Disappearance

Webb helps reconstruct a stellar merger, while competing explanations for M31-2014-DS1 show why fading light needs careful interpretation.

ADDED NOVEMBER 18, 2026

Two objects, two different investigations

The screenshots combine separate Andromeda events. Bao, formally AT 2025abao, is a stellar-merger case reported in November 2026. M31-2014-DS1 is a different source whose fading has prompted competing interpretations.

Bao: a companion spirals into a giant

NASA’s November 18 release describes a small companion merging with an extended supergiant. Modelling estimates the original stars at roughly seven and 0.7 solar masses. The giant’s radius was about 350 times the Sun’s. Once the companion contacted its outer layers, the inferred inspiral lasted about seven orbits, or seven years.

This is common-envelope evolution: the companion moves through the giant’s gaseous envelope and loses orbital energy through drag. The eruption was detected in October 2025; Webb observed the aftermath on December 24, 2025. A November 2026 report date does not mean the event occurred that month.

For scale: Seven solar masses and 350 solar radii are different measurements. A star can be enormously extended without having hundreds of times the Sun’s mass. Likewise, a reconstructed seven-year inspiral does not mean Webb filmed every orbit.

M31-2014-DS1: what does “vanished” mean?

A February 2026 Science study led by Kishalay De interpreted this source as a massive star collapsing into a black hole without a conventional bright supernova. Caltech’s account describes an infrared rise beginning in 2014 and severe subsequent fading. Hubble observations showed visible light approximately 10,000 times fainter.

That ratio means only about 0.01% of the earlier visible brightness remained. It concerns one wavelength range, not necessarily the same reduction in all energy emitted.

The competing explanation deserves space

Beasor and colleagues’ 2026 MNRAS paper used Webb, the Submillimeter Array and Chandra. They found a persistent mid-infrared source and modelled a star obscured by an asymmetric dust distribution. They reported no detected X-ray emission and argued against the particular black-hole accretion explanation for the late light. A stellar merger was also considered plausible.

This does not make a black hole impossible in every model. It means the interpretation is disputed, and a definitive statement that Webb confirmed a silent collapse would overstate the published evidence.

MKP reflection: disappearing from one view is not the whole story

A lamp behind a curtain may become hard to see without switching off. The analogy captures obscuration, although real stellar dust absorbs and re-emits energy in a much more complex way. Astronomers therefore compare wavelengths and track changes over time instead of treating one dim image as a complete diagnosis.

The broader significance is methodological: a dramatic headline becomes a stronger learning opportunity when readers can compare the measurement, the physical model and the alternatives. Observations constrain explanations; further observations can distinguish them.

Discussion question: What later measurement would most help separate an obscured surviving star from a newly formed black hole? Why would a trend across several wavelengths be more informative than one photograph?
